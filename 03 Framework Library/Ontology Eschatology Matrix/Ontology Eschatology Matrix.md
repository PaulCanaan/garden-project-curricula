# Ontology Eschatology Matrix

## Purpose

The Ontology–Eschatology Matrix makes hidden assumptions visible. Every practice implies something about what is real and what a person is; every practice also directs life toward some imagined future. Christian discernment asks whether both the assumed reality and the desired end are faithful to creation and new creation in Christ.

## Axes

- **Ontology:** What is real now? What is a human being, a body, a community, creation, work, power, or technology?
- **Eschatology:** What future is being desired, promised, rehearsed, or produced? What counts as flourishing or completion?

## Quadrants

### Thin ontology, thin eschatology — consumption

Persons are treated as preferences or data, and the future is merely more convenience, stimulation, or control.

### Thick ontology, thin eschatology — preservation

Creation, embodiment, and tradition are honored, but the horizon may be nostalgia, stability, or maintenance without renewal or mission.

### Thin ontology, thick eschatology — utopian abstraction

A compelling future is promised, but present persons, bodies, places, limits, or histories are treated as disposable material.

### Thick ontology, thick eschatology — faithful participation

Created reality is received as meaningful and fallen, while action is oriented toward resurrection, reconciliation, justice, communion, and the renewal of all things in Christ.

## Discernment sequence

1. Describe the practice or institution without slogans.
2. Name its view of persons, bodies, community, creation, power, and agency.
3. Name the future it makes desirable and the losses it normalizes.
4. Compare both with creation, incarnation, cross, resurrection, church, and new creation.
5. Preserve what is true, resist what deforms, and redesign toward faithful participation.

## Biblical and theological grounding

- Genesis 1–2 — the goodness, order, limits, and vocation of creation
- John 1:1–18 — incarnation and the material disclosure of divine life
- 1 Corinthians 15 — bodily resurrection as the Christian future
- Colossians 1:15–20 — creation and reconciliation held together in Christ
- Revelation 21–22 — renewed creation, communion, city, garden, and healing

## Worked example

Evaluating an AI tool:

- **Ontology:** Does it treat people as embodied agents and neighbors, or merely as data and output consumers?
- **Eschatology:** Does it direct work toward wise service and cultivated capacity, or toward frictionless replacement and limitless production?
- **Redesign:** retain assistance that strengthens stewardship; add boundaries that preserve attention, authorship, accountability, and human relationship.

## Limits and failure modes

- The matrix can expose assumptions but cannot settle every practical tradeoff.
- “Thick” does not automatically mean Christian or good.
- Eschatological language must not justify harmful means for a promised end.
- Use alongside Scripture, empirical evidence, affected communities, and domain expertise.
