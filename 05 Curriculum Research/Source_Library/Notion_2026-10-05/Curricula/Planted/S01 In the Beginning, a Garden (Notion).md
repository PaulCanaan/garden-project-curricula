# There Is a Garden Named Delight (part 1)

## In the Beginning, a Garden

> In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth. (Genesis 1:1)¹

Before the Farmer went out to sow seeds, He already had a garden. God is the ultimate farmer and gardener, and the story of the Bible invites us into His garden. Long before our hands touched the soil, God was already at work—speaking light into darkness, bringing order out of chaos, filling emptiness with life, and cultivating a world where His creatures could flourish. The heavens and the earth, with all their beauty and splendor, declare the glory of their Maker (Psalm 19:1). Creation itself is an extravagant gift from a generous and delightful Creator. Out of His abundance, God planted a garden within that good and beautiful creation. With the soil of our hearts prepared and the Scripture open before us, let's step into the story of the garden.

Once I heard a pastor say: "If you don't believe the first chapter of the Bible, it would be hard for you to believe the rest of it." I was a teenager when I heard that statement, and somehow it lingered in my young mind. Ever since then, I have developed a conviction to take the Bible seriously and begun my journey of studying the Bible. I can't remember much about the background of that pastor, but by quoting him and saying "believing", I don't mean "receiving without thinking" or "taking everything literally". Rather, believing is a starting point, as St. Augustine famously put it: "Believe in order to understand; understand in order to believe."² Think of faith like a muscle, and studying the Bible as a training ground. Just as muscles grow stronger by being stretched under weight, faith grows as we wrestle with what we believe and seek to understand it more deeply. God initiates our growth by revealing Himself to us first. We then exercise the God-given gift of reason to seek understanding, and through that wrestling, our capacity for faith grows.

Since I first turned my attention to the opening chapters of the Bible, Genesis 1 and 2 have never failed to teach me something new every time I study them, which is exciting. At the same time, we have to admit that few passages raise more questions to readers than Genesis 1 and 2. I had so many questions, and here are a few of them: How did the plants survive before the sun was created? Why are there two creation accounts? Are they contradictory or complementary to each other? Did God ever create water? It seems like the narrative assumes that "the waters" already existed before the creation… Did the creation happen literally in a week, or through thousands of years? Bible scholars have been debating the young-earth and old-earth theories for centuries.

I hope those questions are not too overwhelming, and we will soon find out that the creation story is full of grace and truth. One thing about truth is that it's not afraid of challenges or questions. Some questions can be addressed earlier, while others may reveal themselves during the process of discovery. Let's take a look at our first question: Was water created? It's an interesting question because the Bible never mentions God saying: "Let there be water…" According to Genesis 1:6, God spoke the sky into existence, separating water from water. And in verse 9, the gathering of the water became seas. So, "water" was "separated" and "gathered", not explicitly "created"? However, if we ask a follow-up question: "Which water?", we can start to see a physical layer as well as a symbolic layer. On the material level, I believe water (H2O) was created, because Genesis 1:1 summarizes the entire creation account: "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth." God created everything, including water. On the literary-theological level, water is a major motif in the biblical worldview that represents chaos and danger. Genesis 1:2 depicts the pre-creation state with the Hebrew words: *tohu wa-bohu*, which is usually translated as "formless and void". Rather than an empty canvas, it's a state that is without order. By describing the Spirit of God hovering over the waters, the text portrays God bringing order over chaos. Therefore, water (as a material) was created, chaos (as a rival force) was not.

The contemporary Western interpretation of the creation account is usually entangled with the endless faith-and-science debate, so I think it would be helpful to consider the next question: Was the 7-day creation a period of seven 24-hour days, or did it span over thousands of years? Elsewhere in the Bible, the relationship between time and the eternal God is referred to as: "With the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day." (2 Peter 3:8; cf. Psalm 90:4). Could that be God making thousands of years look like a week, and an ancient creation look very young? The other side would argue back: How do you explain the reframing phrase "There was an evening, and there was a morning"? My answer to the Day-Age debate is simple: I don't know. I think both sides of the young-earth and old-earth theories have legitimate reasons to support their views. What matters is not the certainty over specific answers, but the heart posture of pursuing truth. Often, the state of "I don't know" is more like "we don't know *yet*". As 1 Corinthians 13:12 reminds us: "For now we see only a reflection as in a mirror, but then face to face. Now I know in part, but then I will know fully, as I am fully known." So, I don't know, I'm not a geologist, and the Bible is not a science textbook. It might not tell us the *age of the rocks*, but it does reveal the heart of the *Rock of Ages*!

If you're still with me, here's why these opening questions matter. I hope wrestling with those two questions has warmed up your mind and spirit and prepared you for the deep dive we are about to take. The first verse of the Bible really is the foundation for everything else: "In the beginning, God…" We do not find ourselves on the first page. Instead, we find the beginning of history itself, and an eternal God who is beyond the dimension of time. Christian philosopher Christopher Watkin comments: "The fact that the universe has a beginning means that reality is shaping up remarkably like a story."³ Genesis 1:1 takes us to the origin of all things, and its second half—"God created the heavens and the earth"—points to a theory of Reality that theologians call "the Creator-creature distinction". There is the self-contained God, and there is the created universe. The Creator-creature distinction is significant because it points out that God is not just bigger, better, smarter, or stronger than us, but He is Other to all creatures. This is the holiness of the Creator: transcendent and set apart. There are ultimately two categories—God and everything else. Therefore, God has no peers and no rivals. There was never any god apart from Him, nor will there ever be.⁴ Transcendence is one of the first impressions Genesis gives us of the Creator.

Have you ever noticed that the names of God are slightly different in Genesis 1 and 2? The name used for God in Genesis 1:1–2:3 is *Elohim,* while throughout the rest of Genesis 2 it becomes "LORD God," or *Yahweh Elohim*. Depending on context, the Hebrew term *elohim* can refer to God or to other heavenly beings; whereas *Yahweh* is the personal covenant name of the God of Israel. Grammatically, the Hebrew word *elohim* is plural in form (the singular form is *El*), which is why it can refer to "gods" in general. But what is worth noticing is that when *Elohim* refers to the God of Israel, it always pairs with singular verbs. The names of God already indicate two perspectives on the same Creator and His relationship to the creation: in Genesis 1, we encounter God as the Cosmic Creator; in Genesis 2, we will meet him as the Personal Caretaker. Different perspectives can reveal the same reality. Imagine flying into Denver. From 30,000 feet, you look out the window and take a photo of the city below. The next day, you walk through downtown Denver and take another photo at street level. The two pictures may look completely different, yet they are pictures of the same Denver. In much the same way, the author of Genesis intentionally gives us different lenses, which stretch our imagination. Before we step into the garden for a close look in Genesis 2, let's first zoom out and take in the panoramic view of the creation.

The literary structure of Genesis 1 is unlike almost anything else in the Bible, which makes it function as an introduction to the Pentateuch (the first five books in the Bible) and even to the larger narrative of the entire Bible. We also cannot ignore the poetic nature of the ancient Hebrew language. Rather than simply listing what God made, Genesis 1 presents creation through rhythm, repetition, symmetry, and intentionally ordered movement. If we read only for information, we can easily miss the beauty of the way the story is told. When I was first introduced to the parallel pattern of the six days of creation, it blew my mind (Thank you, Dr. Tim Mackie and The Bible Project). The pattern goes like this: during the first three days, God separates and forms the realms of the heavens and the earth; during the next three days, He fills those realms with their inhabitants. Day 1 parallels Day 4, Day 2 parallels Day 5, and Day 3 parallels Day 6⁵:

| **Separating (Forming)** | **Realm Formed** | **Filling** | **Realm Filled** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Light separated from darkness | Day 4 | Sun, moon, stars filled the heavenly realm |
| Day 2 | Sky separated from the waters | Day 5 | Birds and sea creatures filled the sky and the seas |
| Day 3 | Dry land separated from the seas; vegetation appears | Day 6 | Land animals filled the land; humanity created |

Put the seven days together, and the movement looks something like this: ABC → A′B′C′ → D. And there are rhythms within the rhythm. Ten times we hear "Then God said," and seven times God looks upon His work and declares it "good" or "very good." Seven, a number often associated with completeness in the Bible, is fitting here as creation is brought to its fullness. Then comes the seventh day, and suddenly the rhythm changes. God does not create anything new. Instead, He rests. Even the familiar evening-and-morning refrain that marks each of the six days is nowhere to be found. The story slows down and draws our attention to this final day.

As a reptile keeper, I know a little bit of that "very good" feeling. One of my favorite things to do is build bioactive terrariums for my geckos. I start with the substrate, then add the plants, arrange the lighting and heating, and introduce tiny decomposers as the clean-up crew. At every step, I stop and make sure everything is good before moving on to the next. But no matter how beautiful the enclosure becomes, you can hardly call it a gecko terrarium without a gecko, right? So finally, the gecko moves in. I lean back in my chair and watch her explore her new home—climbing, hiding, investigating every corner—and with a smile on my face, I think, "Now this is very good." Have you ever had that feeling after finishing something you poured a lot of time and effort into? You step back, take it all in, and smile: *This is good. This is really good.*

But here the analogy breaks down beautifully: I build terrariums for my geckos, but I don't move in with them. God does. He moves into the world He created and makes His dwelling among His creatures. That is where the movement of Genesis 1 has been leading: not simply to a finished world, but to a cosmos filled with the presence and glory of God. The seventh day is the climax of the creation story: the Creator delights in His completed work and enters His Sabbath rest, blessing the seventh day and declaring it holy (Genesis 2:3). By establishing the Sabbath Day, God teaches His people a healthy rhythm of work and rest. When we cease from work, we get to reflect and appreciate the beauty of the creation. Jewish theologian Abraham Joshua Heschel's poetic words are fitting here: "It is a day on which we are called upon to share in what is eternal in time, to turn from the results of creation to the mystery of creation; from the world of creation to the creation of the world."⁶

If the Sabbath is the climax of creation, then humanity is the crown of creation. Throughout the creation story, all the living creatures are created "according to their kinds" (Genesis 1:11–25). However, when we arrive at Genesis 1:26–28, the pattern changes. Instead of making humanity "according to their kinds", God said: "Let us make mankind in our image, according to our likeness." (Genesis 1:26 NIV). The "us" here most likely pictures God addressing His heavenly court rather than being a direct reference to the Trinity.⁷ Yet Christians reading Genesis in light of the overarching narrative of the Bible can still recognize a Trinitarian pattern in the creation story: God creates through His Word while His Spirit hovers over the waters. The emphasis in Genesis 1:26, however, falls on humanity and the extraordinary identity God gives us.

As Old Testament scholar Richard Middleton emphasizes in *The Liberating Image*, the *imago Dei* is not primarily about some invisible quality we possess, but a status and calling we receive: a royal vocation to represent God's rule within His world.⁸ That is why the identity of humanity as image-bearers flows immediately into the commission to "rule" over the creatures and to "fill the earth and subdue it" (Genesis 1:28). Identity and vocation are inseparable. The biblical understanding of "ruling" is nothing like the permission to exploit the earth, but rather, the responsibility of a loving stewardship—to cultivate creation and draw out its God-given potential. You may wonder: If creation is already "very good," what is left for humanity to do? Think of a newborn baby. A parent can look at that child and say, "She is perfect," without meaning, "She should never grow." Her goodness does not exclude growth, but contains the potential for it. In the same way, God creates a world full of possibilities and entrusts humanity, as His vice-regents, with the task of cultivating them, male and female alike (Genesis 1:27). Before anything we accomplish or any status we acquire, the extraordinary worth of humanity is generously given by God. Indeed, being the *imago Dei* is both the ultimate dignity and the great vocation of being human.

Now we have the full picture of the creation order: God is the Creator and Sustainer of all things, distinct from yet present to the creation that depends upon Him. Humanity, uniquely made in God's image, is bestowed with a royal-priestly vocation: to serve God within His world and to represent God's loving rule by tending and cultivating the rest of creation. Creation, in turn, reveals the glory and wisdom of its Creator and provides for the flourishing of life within it. When God, humanity, and creation dwell together in right relationship—each flourishing according to God's good design—we see the biblical picture of harmony and wholeness, condensed into one word: *shalom*.
