### Lesson 1.1 – From Adam to Noah: The Broken Image

#### Key Texts:

* **Genesis 1:26–31** – The original blessing and mandate  
* **Genesis 3:1–24** – The fall and the beginning of exile  
* **Romans 5:12–21** – Sin and grace contrasted in Adam and Christ

**Theme:** Sin enters the world, but God’s purpose in creation continues

#### Part 1: God’s Original Purpose (Genesis 1:26–31)

**Read:** Genesis 1:26–31

Have two readers—one for God’s speech (“Let us make…”) and one for the narrative description—to emphasize both divine intention and execution.

##### Key Observations:

1. **God’s Deliberation** – “Let us make…” (v.26) shows intentionality and relationality in creation.  
2. **Image of God** – Equal dignity and worth for all humans (v.26–27).  
3. **Male and Female** – Designed for relational complementarity, both fully in God’s image (v.27).  
4. **Blessing & Cultural Mandate** – Be fruitful, multiply, fill, subdue, and rule (v.28).  
5. **Provision** – God gives plants for food (v.29–30).  
6. **Very Good** – God’s creation complete and harmonious (v.31).

##### Key Concepts:

1. Humanity Created in God’s Image (Imago Dei)

* **"Let us make man in our image, after our likeness"** introduces the unique status of human beings among all creation.  
* *Image* and *likeness* indicate a special reflection of God’s nature—rationality, moral capacity, relational ability, creativity, and stewardship.  
* This sets the foundation for human dignity and worth.  
* The “image of God” role mirrors a covenant structure: God is the sovereign, humans are His vassals, creation is the realm of governance.  
  2. Dominion and Stewardship  
* God grants humanity **dominion** over fish, birds, livestock, and all the earth.  
* Dominion here is not exploitation, but **responsible care**—acting as God’s representatives to cultivate, protect, and order creation.  
* This is the origin of the biblical concept of creation care.  
  3. Creation of Male and Female  
* Humanity is created **male and female** (v.27), both equally bearing the image of God.  
* Gender is presented as intentional and part of God’s good design for relational and communal life.  
  4. The Blessing and Mandate  
* God **blesses** humanity with fruitfulness: “Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth and subdue it.”  
* The mandate is both about population growth and exercising wise governance over creation.  
* This is often called the **Cultural Mandate** and is seen as humanity’s first calling—to fill the earth with God’s glory through godly offspring, wise rule, and faithful stewardship.  
* This mandate anticipates later covenants (Noahic, Abrahamic) that re-emphasize fruitful multiplication and blessing to the nations.  
  5. Provision from Creation  
* God provides plants and fruit as food for humanity, and plants for animals.  
* This shows God’s care and provision for all living things.  
* At this stage, food sources are plant-based, reflecting the peace and harmony of the original creation.  
* The peaceful provision in Eden points forward to the covenant blessings promised in later redemptive history—rest, abundance, and shalom.  
  6. God’s Declaration of “Very Good”  
* After creating humanity, God looks at all that He has made and declares it **“very good”**—a step above the “good” of earlier creation days.  
* This affirms the inherent goodness of the created order when functioning as God intended.  
  7. Theological Significance  
* Human identity: Defined by God’s image, not by social status, ability, or achievement.  
* Purpose: To represent God’s rule on earth.  
* Relationship: Both vertical (with God) and horizontal (with creation and each other) relationships are built into creation’s design.

##### Group Discussion:

* What does it mean for *you* personally to be made in the image of God?  
* How should the truth of “equal image-bearing” shape the way we treat others?  
* In what ways do we fulfill or neglect our role as creation’s caretakers today?  
* How does the creation mandate anticipate God’s covenant promises to Abraham (Gen 12:1–3)?  
* How does the Creation Mandate shape how we view work, relationships, and the world?  
* How does seeing this as a *covenant beginning* affect your understanding of God’s plan for the world?  
* Where do we see this design broken or distorted today?

##### Gospel Connection:

* Jesus is the **perfect image of God** (Colossians 1:15; Hebrews 1:3).  
* Through His death and resurrection, He restores our ability to fulfill the role Adam failed to keep—representing God faithfully and ruling in righteousness.  
* The New Creation (Revelation 21–22) is the full realization of the covenant purposes started here in Genesis 1.

#### Part 2: The Fall – Sin Breaks the Covenant (Genesis 3:1–24)

**Read:** Genesis 3:1–24

Have different readers for the serpent, Adam, Eve, and God to make the narrative more vivid.

##### Key Concepts:

1. The Serpent’s Deception (vv.1–5)

* **Introduction of the serpent** – crafty and shrewd, a creature God made but one who twists His words.  
* **Twisting God’s command** – the serpent questions (“Did God really say…?”) and denies God’s warning about death.  
* **Temptation pattern** – undermining trust in God’s goodness, appealing to desire for wisdom and autonomy.  
  * Reflects the pattern of temptation: doubt → desire → disobedience.  
* Theologically, this reveals the *origin of human rebellion* and the spiritual dimension of evil.

2. The First Sin and Its Nature (v.6)

* Eve sees the fruit as *good for food*, *pleasing to the eye*, and *desirable for gaining wisdom*—a threefold appeal (physical, aesthetic, intellectual).  
* Adam participates knowingly; sin here is a **willful rejection of God’s word** in favor of self-rule.  
* This is the root of all sin: exchanging God’s authority for our own.

3. Immediate Consequences: Shame and Fear (vv.7–8)

* Eyes opened → awareness of nakedness → shame.  
* They hide from God, showing broken fellowship with Him.  
* Sin fractures both vertical (with God) and horizontal (with each other) relationships.

4. God’s Confrontation and Judgment (vv.9–19)

* **God seeks them out** (“Where are you?”) – showing grace even in judgment.  
* **Accountability** – each is questioned and held responsible.  
  * Judgment tailored to each: serpent (defeat), woman (pain, relational strife), man (toil, death).  
* **Curses and consequences**:  
  * **Serpent** – humiliation, perpetual enmity with the woman’s offspring.  
  * **Protoevangelium** (v.15) – promise of a coming seed who will crush the serpent’s head (first gospel announcement).  
  * **Woman** – pain in childbirth, relational tension with husband.  
  * **Man** – cursed ground, toil, and eventual death.

5. Exile from Eden (vv.20–24)

* God clothes them with **garments of skin**—a gracious covering, likely the first death as a substitute, foreshadowing sacrifice.  
* Banishment from Eden prevents access to the tree of life—judgment mixed with mercy, avoiding eternal life in a fallen state.  
* Cherubim and a flaming sword guard the way back—symbolizing holiness and separation from God’s presence.

6. Theological Significance

* **Sin’s universal pattern**: doubt God’s word → desire autonomy → disobedience → alienation → judgment.  
* **God’s mercy amid judgment**: pursuit, promise of redemption, provision of covering.  
  * Judgment: curses disrupt the blessings of the creation mandate.  
  * Mercy: God promises a redeemer (seed of the woman), initiating the covenant promise of salvation that runs through Scripture.  
* **Covenantal rupture**: Adam’s covenant role as God’s image-bearer and ruler is corrupted, setting the stage for later covenants to restore God’s purpose.  
* **Christological thread**: The promised seed points directly to Jesus  
  * Genesis 3:15 begins God’s redemptive plan: a lineage leading to Christ, the covenant mediator who restores what Adam lost.

##### Discussion Questions:

* In what ways do we see the same temptation pattern from Genesis 3 in our lives today?  
* How does God’s question, “Where are you?” shape your view of His character?  
* What does the promise of the “seed” teach us about God’s covenant purposes?  
* How do shame and hiding still affect our relationship with God and others?  
* What stands out to you about God’s response to their sin?  
* Why is it important to see God’s judgment and mercy together in this passage?  
* What hope is offered in the promise of the serpent-crusher?

##### Gospel Connection:

* Jesus is the promised offspring who crushes the serpent (Hebrews 2:14, 1 John 3:8).  
* His perfect obedience repairs Adam’s failure, inaugurating the new covenant in His blood.  
* In Christ, believers regain access to God’s presence (symbolized by Eden restored in Revelation 21–22).

#### Part 3: Adam and Christ – Two Representatives (Romans 5:12–21)

**Read:** Romans 5:12–21

##### Key Concepts:

1. Sin and Death through Adam (vv.12–14)

* **Adam as representative head**: Sin entered the world through *one man* (Adam), and death came as the consequence.  
* **Universal effect**: Death spread to all because all sinned—Adam’s sin implicates humanity as a whole.  
  * Adam as federal head of humanity—his trespass implicates the whole human race.  
* **Law’s role**: Sin existed before the Law of Moses, but without the law sin was not charged in the same way—yet death still reigned.  
  * Even before the Mosaic Law, death reigned—showing sin’s universal grip.  
* Adam is presented as a **type** (foreshadowing) of Christ.

2. Christ as the Second Adam (vv.15–17)

* **Contrast between Adam and Christ**:  
  * *Adam’s trespass* brought death.  
  * *Christ’s gift* brought grace and life.  
* Grace is greater than sin—God’s response in Christ far surpasses the damage caused by Adam.  
* Through Christ, believers reign in life rather than being reigned over by death.  
  * Christ, as head of the *new covenant*, reverses Adam’s failure and secures righteousness for His people.

3. The Two Representatives and Their Results (vv.18–19)

* **One trespass → condemnation for all** (Adam).  
* **One act of righteousness → justification and life for all** (Christ).  
* Through disobedience of one man many were made sinners; through *obedience* of one man many are made righteous.  
* This is the doctrine of federal headship—Adam and Christ as covenant representatives.

4. Law’s Purpose and Grace’s Triumph (vv.20–21)

* The Law came in to **increase trespass**—making sin more visible and accountable.  
* Where sin increased, **grace increased all the more**.  
* Grace reigns through righteousness, leading to eternal life through Jesus Christ.

5. Theological Significance

* **Covenantal headship**: Adam as the head of the old humanity, Christ as the head of the new humanity.  
* **Justification**: Christ’s obedience is credited to believers, reversing Adam’s curse.  
* **Victory of grace**: God’s redemptive plan is not merely a repair but an overwhelming triumph.  
* **Union with Christ**: Believers participate in the benefits of Christ’s righteous act because He represents them.

##### Discussion Questions:

* How does Paul’s teaching about Adam and Christ help you understand the seriousness of sin?  
* Why is it good news that Christ represents us before God?  
* In what ways does seeing Christ as the “new Adam” deepen your appreciation for His obedience?  
* How does the abundance of grace in Christ shape the way we face our ongoing struggle with sin?  
* How does this passage tie together the story of God’s covenants from Genesis to Jesus?

##### Gospel Connection:

* Jesus is the true and better Adam who resists temptation, obeys perfectly, and secures life for all who trust in Him.  
* His obedience is credited to believers just as Adam’s sin was counted against humanity.  
* The new covenant brings a new creation (2 Corinthians 5:17) and restores what was lost in Eden.

#### Reflection & Application

**Personal Reflection Questions:**

* Where do I see myself in the story of Adam and Eve?  
* How have I experienced the consequences of sin in my life?  
* In what ways do I struggle to live as God’s image-bearer today?  
* Where do I need to receive and rest in God’s abounding grace?

**Group Prayer Ideas:**

* Confess areas where we’ve doubted God’s goodness or distorted His image.  
* Thank God for sending Jesus as our better representative.  
* Ask for strength to live faithfully as His image-bearers in our communities.

#### Suggested Follow-Up or Homework

* **Memory Verse:** Romans 5:17 — *“If, because of one man's trespass, death reigned through that one man, much more will those who receive the abundance of grace... reign in life through the one man Jesus Christ.”*

* **Personal Journaling Prompt:**  
   Reflect on an area of your life where you are tempted to “be like God” — trusting your judgment more than God’s voice. How can you practice surrender this week?

### Lesson 1.2 – Be Fruitful and Multiply: A World Preserved

#### Key Texts:

* **Genesis 6:9–22** — Noah’s righteousness and God’s command  
* **Genesis 8:20–9:17** — The covenant with Noah and the sign of the rainbow  
* **Isaiah 54:9–10** — God’s enduring mercy and unshakable covenant

**Theme:** *God’s covenant with Noah guarantees creation's stability, preparing the world for redemption.*

#### Part 1: God Remembers Noah (Genesis 6:9–22)

**Read:** Genesis 6:9–22

Assign three readers—one for God’s description of the world (vv.11–13), one for the ark instructions (vv.14–21), and one for Noah’s obedience (v.22)—to emphasize the narrative flow.

##### Key Concepts:

1. Noah’s Righteousness (v.9)

* **“Noah was a righteous man, blameless among the people of his time, and he walked faithfully with God.”**  
* *Righteous* here means being in right relationship with God through faith and obedience (cf. Hebrews 11:7).  
* *Blameless* does not mean sinless, but upright in conduct and distinct from the corrupt generation.  
* Parallels Adam’s original role—walking with God and representing Him.

2. Human Corruption and Violence (v.11–12)

* The earth is “corrupt” and “full of violence” due to human wickedness.  
* Creation mandate (Genesis 1:26–28) has been reversed—humanity now spreads violence rather than God’s glory.  
  * Humanity’s role as image-bearers and stewards is perverted.  
* God’s assessment shows total moral decay: “all flesh had corrupted their way.”

3. Divine Judgment Announced (v.13)

* God declares that the end of all flesh is near—He will destroy the earth because of human corruption.  
* This is a covenant lawsuit moment: God, as covenant Lord, enforces the sanctions for disobedience.

4. God’s Covenant with Noah (v.18)

* The first explicit mention of “covenant” (Hebrew *berit*) in Scripture.  
* God promises to establish His covenant with Noah—preserving him, his family, and representatives of all living creatures.  
* This covenant is rooted in God’s grace and is the means by which His creation purposes will continue.

5. Ark Instructions and Obedience (vv.14–16, 22)

* Detailed architectural plans for the ark show God’s **intentionality** in salvation.  
* Noah’s role is to follow God’s word exactly.  
* Verse 22 emphasizes total obedience—“Noah did all that God commanded him.”  
  * A model of faith expressed in action.  
* This mirrors Adam’s original calling, but Noah succeeds where Adam failed (at least in this moment).

6. Preservation of Life (vv.19–21)

* Pairs of every creature are to be brought into the ark to preserve species.  
* Food provision for humans and animals underscores God’s care for His creation.  
* This anticipates a re-commissioning after the flood—like a new creation.

7. Theological Significance

* **Covenantal Continuity**:   
  * God’s redemptive plan is preserved through one man and his family.  
  * Noah is a kind of “new Adam”—walking with God, stewarding creation, receiving a mandate to preserve life.  
* **Judgment and Salvation**:   
  * The flood is both an act of judgment on sin and an act of deliverance for the faithful.  
  * Without God’s intervention through Noah, humanity (and the plan of redemption) would be wiped out.  
* **Typology**:   
  * The ark prefigures Christ—God’s appointed means of salvation from judgment.  
  * Just as the ark was God’s appointed means of rescue from judgment, Christ is God’s ultimate provision for salvation from sin’s judgment.  
  * Just as those inside the ark were saved from judgment waters, those in Christ are saved from God’s final judgment.  
* **Obedience of Faith**:   
  * Noah’s faith is demonstrated by meticulous obedience to God’s word.

##### Discussion Questions:

* What does it mean that Noah was “righteous” and “blameless” in his generation?  
* How do you see parallels between Noah’s world and our own?  
* Why is it important that God’s covenant promise came before Noah built the ark?  
* How does Noah’s obedience challenge or encourage your walk with God?  
* In what ways does the ark point forward to Christ as our refuge?

##### Gospel Connection:

* **Noah as a type of Christ**:   
  * Both provide salvation for their people through obedience to God’s will.  
* **The ark as a symbol of union with Christ**:   
  * Just as those inside the ark were saved from judgment waters, those in Christ are saved from God’s final judgment.  
* The Noahic covenant preserves the world for the coming of the ultimate covenant mediator - Jesus.

#### Part 2: God's Covenant and the Rainbow (Genesis 8:20–9:17)

**Read:** Genesis 8:20–22 and 9:1–17

Have one person read 8:20–22 (worship and promise), another read 9:1–7 (mandate and instructions), and another read 9:8–17 (covenant and sign).

##### Key Concepts:

1. Noah’s Worship After Deliverance (8:20–21)

* **Altar and sacrifice** – Noah’s first act after leaving the ark is to worship God.  
* Sacrifices of “clean” animals are a response of gratitude and devotion.  
* God’s response: He is pleased with the offering and resolves **never again to curse the ground** because of humanity, despite human sinfulness.  
  2. God’s Promise of Creation Stability (8:22)  
* Regular cycles—seedtime, harvest, cold, heat, summer, winter, day, night—will continue as long as the earth endures.  
* This is a covenant of stability, ensuring that creation will not again be destroyed in total by flood.  
* It provides the necessary environment for God’s redemptive plan to move forward.  
  3. Renewal of the Creation Mandate (9:1–7)  
* **Blessing and fruitfulness** – “Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth,” echoing Genesis 1:28.  
* Humanity’s role as rulers over creation is reaffirmed, but with changes:  
  * Fear of humans is placed upon animals.  
  * Human diet expanded to include meat, but blood is prohibited (symbolizing life).  
* **Sanctity of human life** – Murder is prohibited; shedding human blood requires a reckoning because humans are made in God’s image.  
* This affirms human dignity and introduces basic principles of justice.  
  4. Establishment of the Noahic Covenant (9:8–11)  
* God makes an everlasting covenant with Noah, his descendants, and every living creature.  
* Promise: Never again will a flood destroy all life or the earth.  
* This covenant is unconditional—grounded in God’s promise, not human obedience.  
  5. The Covenant Sign: The Rainbow (9:12–17)  
* The rainbow is the sign of the covenant, a visible reminder for God and humanity of His promise.  
* The sign assures creation that God’s wrath will not again be expressed in a global flood.  
* It’s a universal covenant—not just with God’s people, but with all living beings.  
  6. Theological Significance  
* **Worship before mission**:   
  * Noah’s worship sets the tone for renewed life after salvation.  
* **Covenantal stability**:   
  * God commits to preserve the world so His plan of redemption can unfold through history.  
  * The renewal of the mandate ties back to Genesis 1, showing God’s purpose for humanity is unchanged despite sin.  
* **Human dignity and justice**:  
  * Sanctity of life rooted in the image of God.  
* **Universal scope**:  
  * This covenant includes all creation, pointing to God’s care for the entire cosmos.  
  * Unlike later covenants tied to a particular people, this covenant is with all creation—foreshadowing the cosmic scope of redemption.  
* **Sign of the covenant**:   
  * The rainbow as a tangible expression of God’s faithfulness.  
* **Typology**:  
  * As the ark points to Christ as salvation from judgment, the Noahic covenant points to God’s ultimate plan to bring about a new heavens and new earth.

##### Discussion Questions:

* Why is Noah’s first act after leaving the ark an act of worship?  
* How does God’s promise of creation stability affect our understanding of His faithfulness?  
* What does this passage teach us about the value of human life?  
* Why is it significant that this covenant is made with all creation, not just Noah’s family?  
* How does the rainbow as a covenant sign encourage us when we face fear or uncertainty?  
* Why is it important that this covenant is made not just with Noah, but all creation?  
* What does the rainbow symbolize in this context?  
* How does God’s mercy create space for the redemption story to unfold?

##### Gospel Connection

* The Noahic covenant points forward to a greater covenant through Christ.  
* In Jesus, God not only preserves life from temporal judgment but rescues His people from eternal judgment.  
* Just as the rainbow assures us of God’s mercy after judgment, the cross assures us of God’s mercy through Christ.

#### Part 3: Covenant and Mercy (Isaiah 54:9–10)

**Read:** Isaiah 54:9–10

##### Key Concepts:

1. Comparison to the Days of Noah (v.9)

* God recalls the **Noahic covenant** (Genesis 8:20–9:17), when He promised never again to destroy the earth with a flood.  
* Just as that promise was **binding and universal**, God now pledges that His wrath will not overwhelm His people.  
  * Just as that covenant was binding and irreversible, so is His promise of peace toward His people.  
* This connection shows that God’s post-judgment mercy in Noah’s time foreshadows His post-exile restoration for Israel.  
  * This draws a direct line from creation stability to relational stability.

2. God’s Oath of Peace (v.9)

* The imagery moves from “never again” to the promise **“I will not be angry with you, nor will I rebuke you.”**  
  * This is covenant language—God binding Himself by oath to a posture of peace toward His people.  
  * Context: Isaiah is speaking to a people who will face exile but will also be restored.

3. Unshakable Covenant Love (v.10)

* **Mountains and hills may be removed**—even the most stable features of creation can change—**but God’s steadfast love will not depart**.  
* “Steadfast love” (*hesed*) is covenant loyalty—faithful, merciful love bound by God’s own promise.  
* God’s “covenant of peace” is unbreakable, grounded not in human performance but in divine commitment.

4. Covenant of Peace

* This term anticipates the **new covenant** promises (cf. Ezekiel 37:26), involving restored relationship, security, and flourishing under God’s blessing.  
* It points to ultimate fulfillment in the Messiah, whose death secures peace with God (Isaiah 53:5; Romans 5:1).

5. Theological Significance

* **Continuity of Covenant Mercy**:   
  * Just as the Noahic covenant secured the stability of the world, God’s covenant of peace secures the stability of His relationship with His people.  
  * Though this covenant is not redemptive in itself, it **guarantees the conditions** necessary for God’s redemptive work to unfold through Abraham, Israel, and Christ.  
  * It reflects the **unilateral nature of God’s grace** — no conditions are placed on Noah beyond trusting God and building the ark.  
* **Grace after Judgment**:   
  * The context is restoration after discipline—judgment is not the final word.  
  * Like after the flood, judgment (exile) is not God’s final word—restoration is.  
* **Faithfulness in an Unstable World**:   
  * Even if creation’s most enduring features vanish, God’s promise stands.  
* **Messianic Fulfillment**:   
  * Ultimately realized in Christ, who embodies and secures the covenant of peace.

##### Discussion Questions:

* Why do you think God chose the Noahic covenant as a comparison here?  
* How does the idea of God’s “steadfast love” encourage you in uncertain times?  
* What is the difference between God’s covenant promise and a human promise?  
* How does this passage help you understand the purpose of God’s covenants across the Bible?  
* In what ways does Christ fulfill this “covenant of peace” for us today?  
* What does it say about God’s heart that He gives a visible reminder of His mercy?  
* In what ways is the church called to be a witness of God’s preserving grace today?

##### Gospel Connection:

* The covenant of peace is ultimately established through Jesus, the suffering servant of Isaiah 53, whose death secures peace with God.  
* Just as the rainbow is the sign of God’s post-flood promise, the cross is the sign of God’s ultimate and unbreakable covenant commitment.

#### Reflection & Application

**Personal Reflection Prompts:**

* How have you seen God preserve or protect you even when life has been chaotic?  
* Where might you be called to “build an ark” — to obey even when the cost is high?  
* In what ways can you be a sign of God’s mercy and peace to those around you?

**Group Prayer Ideas:**

* Thank God for His faithfulness and patience with humanity.  
* Pray for hearts willing to obey God even when it’s costly or unclear.  
* Pray for the group to be a witness of God’s mercy in your community.

#### Suggested Follow-Up or Homework

* **Memory Verse:** Genesis 9:13 — *“I have set my bow in the cloud, and it shall be a sign of the covenant between me and the earth.”*

* **Reflection Exercise:**  
   Create a short prayer or poem around the theme of the rainbow as God’s sign of peace. Bring it to share at next week’s study if you’re comfortable.

### Lesson 1.3 – No Longer Destruction: Stability for Redemption

#### Key Texts:

* **2 Peter 3:1–13** — The future day of the Lord and the call to holy living  
* **Romans 8:18–25** — Creation’s groaning and the hope of restoration

Theme: God’s promise to preserve the world prepares the way for redemption.

#### Part 1: God's Covenant Creates Space for Redemption

##### Connection to Genesis 9 (Prior Study)

* In Genesis 9, God establishes the Noahic covenant after the flood, promising never again to destroy the earth by water.   
* This promise is unconditional—  
  * The responsibility of this promise rests entirely on God’s word, not human behavior.   
* The rainbow serves as the sign of this covenant, a visible reminder that, though humanity remains sinful (“the intention of man’s heart is evil from his youth,” Gen 8:21), God has committed Himself to preserve the world.  
  * This is more than divine restraint; it’s intentional patience.   
  * God is securing a stable stage for the unfolding of His redemptive drama.   
* Without this covenant, every outbreak of human evil could invite immediate, total destruction.   
  * Instead, God chooses to let history continue so His purposes—especially His promise to bless the nations through the seed of the woman (Gen 3:15) and the line of Abraham—can be fulfilled.   
  * Schreiner emphasizes that the Noahic covenant is a platform covenant:   
    * It preserves creation so that the other covenants (Abrahamic, Mosaic, Davidic, New) can be enacted.

##### Bridge to 2 Peter

* The New Testament deepens this perspective.   
  * 2 Peter 3:3–10 reminds us that God’s past judgment by water points ahead to a future judgment—not by flood, but by fire.   
  * The flood was a type, a preview of the final day when God will fully and finally remove sin from His creation.   
* Peter is clear: the reason judgment hasn’t yet fallen is not because God is indifferent or slow, but because **“The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise… but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance”** (2 Pet 3:9).  
* Just as the rainbow in Noah’s day testified to God’s mercy after judgment, the ongoing rhythm of seasons (Gen 8:22) testifies now to His continued patience.   
  * This delay is space for repentance—an open window in which God calls people to turn from sin and find refuge in Christ before the day of fire comes.   
  * The Noahic covenant thus becomes a living backdrop for our age:   
    * a world preserved, not because it is righteous, but because God is merciful and committed to completing His redemption plan.

#### Part 2: The Day of the Lord and God’s Patience (2 Peter 3:1–13)

**Read:** 2 Peter 3:1–13

Have one reader for vv.1–7 (scoffers and judgment), another for vv.8–10 (God’s timing and the Day of the Lord), and another for vv.11–13 (holy living and hope).

##### Key Concepts:

1. Stirring Up a Pure Mind by Reminder (vv.1–2)

* Peter writes to remind believers of the words spoken by the prophets and the commands of Jesus through the apostles.  
  * Peter stirs up “sincere minds”  
* Faithfulness requires remembering God’s promises and warnings, not just hearing them once.  
* Scripture—Old Testament prophecy and apostolic teaching—is the basis for hope and endurance.

2. The Reality of Scoffers (vv.3–4)

* In the last days, scoffers will follow their sinful desires and mock God’s promise, asking, “Where is the promise of his coming?”  
* They misinterpret the apparent delay as proof God won’t act.  
* This reflects a heart posture of unbelief rooted in desire for self-rule, not genuine doubt seeking truth.

3. Willful Forgetting of God’s Judgment in History (vv.5–7)

* Scoffers deliberately ignore that:  
  1. God created the world by His word.  
  2. God judged the world by water in the days of Noah.  
* Just as the world was once destroyed by flood, it is now reserved for fire—the final purifying judgment and destruction of the ungodly.

4. God’s Timing and Patience (vv.8–9)

* God’s perspective on time is different—“with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day.”  
* The delay is not slowness, but mercy—God is giving opportunity for repentance because He desires that none should perish, reflecting His Covenant patience. 

5. The Day of the Lord (v.10)

* The day will come like a thief—suddenly and unexpectedly.  
* The heavens will pass away, the elements will be dissolved, and the earth’s works will be exposed (or “laid bare” / “burned up” depending on translation).  
* Nothing will escape God’s purifying judgment.

6. Holy Living in Light of the Coming Day (vv.11–12)

* Because the present world will be dissolved, believers are called to holy conduct and godliness, living in expectation of the day of God.  
  * Knowledge of the end motivates godly living now.  
* Christians are to be looking forward to and even “hastening” that day through faithful living and participation in God’s mission.  
  * Hope fixed on the promise of the new heavens and new earth, where righteousness dwells.

7. The New Heavens and New Earth (v.13)

* Believers look forward to the fulfillment of God’s promise: a new creation where righteousness dwells.  
* This is the final goal of redemptive history—restored fellowship with God in a renewed cosmos.

8. Theological Significance

* **Covenantal Continuity**:   
  * The Noahic covenant’s preservation of the world (Genesis 9) allows time for this period of patience and repentance.  
  * This stability is not permanent—it’s temporary until the final day of judgment and renewal.  
  * God’s patience now mirrors His restraint after the flood—delaying judgment to allow repentance.  
* **Judgment and Renewal**:   
  * Final judgment is not merely destructive—it clears the way for the new creation.  
  * The final “covenant goal” is the restored world of righteousness (Revelation 21–22), the consummation of all God’s covenant promises.  
* **Ethics from Eschatology**:   
  * Knowing the future shapes how we live now.  
* **Mission Urgency**:   
  * God’s delay is an open door for gospel proclamation.

##### Discussion Questions:

* Why do you think Peter connects the Flood with final judgment?  
* How does Peter’s reminder about the flood help us take God’s future judgment seriously?  
* What does this passage teach us about God's patience?  
* In what ways have you experienced God’s patience in your own life?  
* Why is it dangerous to mistake God’s delay for indifference?  
* How does the promise of the new heavens and new earth shape your daily decisions?  
* What does it mean to “hasten” the coming day of God (v.12)?  
* How should our lives reflect our hope in the “new heavens and new earth”?

#### Part 3: Creation Groans and Waits for Redemption (Romans 8:18–25)

**Read:** Romans 8:18–25

Have one reader for vv.18–21 (creation’s longing) and another for vv.22–25 (groaning and hope).

##### Key Concepts:

1. Present Suffering vs. Future Glory (v.18)

* Paul declares that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that will be revealed to us.  
* Perspective: The weight of present trials is temporary, while future glory is eternal and surpassing.  
* Glory here involves both the believer’s transformation and the renewal of creation.

2. Creation’s Eager Expectation (vv.19–21)

* Personification of creation:   
  * Creation is pictured as longing and waiting for the revealing of the sons of God (believers in their glorified state).  
* Creation was subjected to futility (frustration, decay) because of human sin (Genesis 3).  
* This subjection was not the end  
  * God subjected it “in hope” that creation itself will be set free from corruption into the freedom of the glory of God’s children.  
* Key idea: Humanity’s redemption is tied to the restoration of the whole created order.

3. Groaning of Creation (v.22)

* Creation is compared to a woman in childbirth—  
  * Suffering is real and intense but purposeful, leading to new life.  
* This groaning has been ongoing “until now,” covering the entire span of the fallen world’s history.

4. Believers’ Groaning (v.23)

* Believers, though they have the “firstfruits of the Spirit,” also groan inwardly.  
  * This groaning comes from living in the “already/not yet” tension  
    * Redeemed but still awaiting the full adoption, which Paul defines here as the **redemption of our bodies** (resurrection)

5. Hope and Patience in Redemption (vv.24–25)

* Salvation is described in terms of hope—not wishful thinking, but confident expectation based on God’s promises.  
  * Hope requires patience—trusting God’s timing for the fullness of redemption.  
* Christian hope looks forward to the complete transformation of both believers and creation.

6. Theological Significance

* **Eschatological Perspective**:   
  * Present suffering is temporary; glory is eternal.  
* **Cosmic Redemption**:   
  * God’s plan includes not only saving individuals but renewing the entire creation.  
  * The new heavens and new earth (Isaiah 65; Revelation 21) are the consummation of the covenant storyline.  
* **Already/Not Yet Tension**:   
  * The Spirit’s presence (“firstfruits”) comes from the new covenant promises (Ezekiel 36; Jeremiah 31).  
  * We have the Spirit as the down payment of redemption, but full realization awaits Christ’s return.  
* **Hope-Driven Perseverance**:   
  * Patient endurance is sustained by confidence in God’s future promises.  
* **Covenantal Link**:   
  * This restoration fulfills God’s creation covenant purpose—humanity ruling over a perfected creation, fully reflecting His glory.

##### Discussion Questions:

* How does Paul’s view of future glory change how we see present suffering?  
* What does it mean that creation is waiting for the “revealing of the sons of God”?  
* How does having the Spirit as the “firstfruits” shape our hope?  
* Why is patience such an important part of biblical hope?  
* How does this passage connect to the promises God made in the earlier covenants?  
* How does Paul describe creation’s relationship to sin and salvation?  
* Why is it significant that God intends to redeem creation, not just souls?  
* What does this passage reveal about the nature of Christian hope?

##### Gospel Connection

* Christ’s resurrection is the first step in the new creation—He is the “firstfruits” of those who will rise (1 Cor. 15:20).  
* His work not only redeems His people but guarantees the renewal of the world.  
  * God’s patience will not last forever; the fire of judgment leads to a re-creation, not annihilation.  
* In Christ, we have both the certainty of future glory and the present empowerment to endure.

#### Reflection & Application

**Personal Reflection Prompts:**

* Where do you see brokenness in the world that makes you long for renewal?  
* Are you more motivated by fear of judgment or the hope of restoration? Why?  
* How can you live a “holy and godly life” in anticipation of God’s promise (2 Peter 3:11)?

**Group Prayer Ideas:**

* Thank God for His patience and mercy in delaying judgment.  
* Pray for endurance and faith to live expectantly, not passively.  
* Lift up creation's brokenness (e.g. environmental, societal, spiritual) and ask for God’s healing and justice.

#### Suggested Follow-Up or Homework

* **Memory Verse:** 2 Peter 3:9 — *“The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish...”*

* **Reflection Exercise:**  
   Write a short letter from the perspective of creation — groaning but hoping. What might the trees, oceans, or sky “say” as they wait for redemption?

### Lesson 2.1 – Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob: God’s Call and Promise

#### Key Texts:

* **Genesis 12:1–9** — The call of Abram and the promise  
* **Genesis 15:1–6** — God's covenant confirmed through faith  
* **Genesis 28:10–22** — God's covenant passed to Jacob

**Theme:** God initiates a covenant with Abraham that unfolds through his descendants, revealing His plan to bless the nations.

#### Part 1: God's Call to Abraham (Genesis 12:1–9)

**Read Together:** Genesis 12:1–9

##### Key Concepts:

1. God’s Call to Abram (v.1)

* Divine initiative:  
  * God calls Abram to leave his country, his people, and his father’s household.  
* The call requires faith:   
  * Stepping away from security and familiarity.  
  * A call into the unknown—trusting God’s word over personal security.  
* Covenant relationships in Scripture often begin with God’s sovereign choice.

2. God’s Covenant Promises (vv.2–3)

* **Nationhood**:  
  * “I will make you into a great nation.” This implies descendants and land.  
* **Blessing** :   
  * God will bless Abram and make his name great.  
  * Elevating Abram’s reputation for God’s purposes.  
* **Mission**:  
  * Abram will be a blessing; all peoples on earth will be blessed through him.  
  * This is the foundational missional promise of Scripture—  
    * God’s covenant plan for global blessing through one chosen family.  
    * “All peoples on earth will be blessed through you” — the global mission of God’s covenant.

3. Abram’s Obedience (v.4–5)

* Abram obeys without knowing all the details (cf. Hebrews 11:8).  
* He takes Sarai, Lot, and all possessions and sets out for Canaan.  
* Faith is expressed in action—obedience is a hallmark of covenant response.

4. Arrival in Canaan (vv.6–7)

* Abram travels through the land to Shechem, a significant future location in Israel’s story.  
* God reaffirms the promise: “To your offspring I will give this land.”  
* Abram responds with worship, building an altar to the Lord.

5. Ongoing Journey and Worship (vv.8–9)

* Abram moves on, pitching tents and building another altar.  
* His journey is marked by worship and calling on God’s name, demonstrating ongoing dependence.

6. Theological Significance

* **Covenantal Foundation**:   
  * This passage is the starting point of the Abrahamic covenant, which shapes the rest of redemptive history.  
* **Faith and Obedience**:   
  * Abram’s journey shows what covenant trust looks like in action.  
* **Blessing to the Nations**:   
  * God’s purpose is not just to bless Abram’s family but to bring blessing to *all* peoples—fulfilled ultimately in Christ (Galatians 3:8, 14).  
* **Land and People**:   
  * Promise of land and descendants connects back to God’s creation purposes in Genesis 1–2 and forward to the new creation.

##### Discussion Questions:

* What stands out about God’s call to Abraham?  
* What does Abram’s willingness to leave home teach us about faith?  
* How do the promises to Abram connect back to God’s purposes in creation (Genesis 1–2)?  
* In what ways does God’s covenant with Abram show His concern for the whole world?  
* How is Abram’s pattern of obedience and worship a model for us today?  
* How do we see the fulfillment of these promises in Jesus?  
* How would it feel to leave behind everything familiar in obedience to God?  
* Why do you think God chose to bless “all families of the earth” through one man?

##### Gospel Connection:

* The promise that “all peoples on earth will be blessed through you” finds its ultimate fulfillment in Jesus, Abraham’s descendant.  
* In Christ, both Jews and Gentiles receive the blessing of justification and the gift of the Spirit (Galatians 3:14).  
* The Abrahamic covenant points to the new covenant, where the blessing is fully realized.

#### Part 2: A Covenant of Faith (Genesis 15:1–6)

**Read:** Genesis 15:1–6

One reader for vv.1–3 (Abram’s doubts) and another for vv.4–6 (God’s response and Abram’s faith).

##### Key Concepts:

1. God’s Reassurance to Abram (v.1)

* “Fear not, Abram” – a divine encouragement, addressing uncertainty or discouragement after earlier challenges (Genesis 14).  
* God identifies Himself as Abram’s shield (protector) and very great reward (ultimate blessing)  
  * Indicating that God Himself is the ultimate blessing, beyond land or descendants.

2. Abram’s Honest Question (vv.2–3)

* Abram voices concern:   
  * Despite God’s promise (Genesis 12), he remains childless.  
* Without an heir, his estate would pass to his servant Eliezer of Damascus.  
* Shows that faith can include honest wrestling with God’s promises.  
  * Faith is not denial of difficulty.  
  * Shows that faith includes wrestling and questioning — Abram brings his doubts directly to God.

3. God’s Specific Promise (v.4)

* God clarifies: Abram’s heir will come from his own body  
  * Direct and miraculous fulfillment, not a workaround.  
* This narrows and intensifies the covenant promise.

4. The Stars as a Sign (v.5)

* God takes Abram outside and points to the stars  
  * God uses a visible, unforgettable image to illustrate the scope of His promise  
    * Descendants as countless as the stars.  
* This visual reinforces the magnitude of God’s covenant promise.  
  * This imagery emphasizes God’s power to do what seems impossible.

5. Abram’s Faith and God’s Righteousness (v.6)

* **“Abram believed the LORD”**—faith here is trust in God’s word despite impossible circumstances.  
* **“And he counted it to him as righteousness”**—God credits faith, not works, as the basis for right standing.  
* This verse becomes a cornerstone for the doctrine of justification by faith (Romans 4; Galatians 3).

6. Theological Significance

* **Faith-Centered Covenant**:   
  * Abram’s trust becomes the model for covenant relationship with God.  
* **Justification by Faith**:   
  * Abram models the covenant principle: righteousness is grounded in faith in God’s word, not human achievement.  
  * Paul cites this verse as proof that righteousness is a gift of grace received through belief, not human effort.  
* **God’s Covenant Faithfulness**:   
  * God reaffirms His promise in the face of human doubt.  
  * God takes initiative to reassure His people when promises seem delayed.  
* **Promise Amid Impossibility**:   
  * God’s power is displayed in fulfilling what is humanly impossible.

##### Discussion Questions:

* What doubts or questions does Abraham express here?  
* What does it mean that Abraham’s faith was “counted as righteousness”?  
* Why do you think God begins His word to Abram with “Do not be afraid”?  
* How does Abram’s question encourage us to bring our own doubts to God?  
* What does v.6 teach us about how someone becomes righteous before God?  
* How does the image of the stars help strengthen our trust in God’s promises?  
* How does Abram’s faith point us forward to the gospel?  
* How is this covenant an early picture of salvation by grace through faith?

##### Gospel Connection

* Just as Abram was declared righteous by trusting God’s promise, we are declared righteous by trusting in Jesus, the promised offspring who fulfills the covenant.  
* The blessing to Abram’s descendants extends to all who have faith in Christ (Galatians 3:7–9, 14).

#### Part 3: Covenant Passed to Jacob (Genesis 28:10–22)

**Read:** Genesis 28:10–22

##### Key Concepts:

1. Jacob’s Situation (v.10–11)

* Jacob is fleeing from Esau after deceiving him (Gen 27).  
* He is vulnerable and alone, sleeping in the open with only a stone for a pillow.  
* Context shows Jacob is not seeking God, but God graciously meets him.  
  * God meets him in his weakness, not his strength.

2. Jacob’s Dream (vv.12–13a)

* He sees a ladder (or stairway) reaching from earth to heaven, with angels ascending and descending.  
* Symbol: connection between heaven and earth  
  * God’s realm and humanity’s realm.  
* God Himself stands above it — showing His sovereignty and presence.

3. God’s Covenant Renewal (vv.13–15)

* God reaffirms to Jacob the Abrahamic promises:  
  * **Land**:   
    * “The land on which you lie I will give to you and your offspring.”  
  * **Offspring**:   
    * Jacob’s descendants will be numerous like dust, spreading in all directions.  
  * **Universal blessing**:   
    * “In you and your offspring shall all the families of the earth be blessed.”  
* God adds personal assurance:  
  * **Presence**: “I am with you.”  
  * **Protection**: “I will keep you wherever you go.”  
  * **Promise**: “I will bring you back to this land.”  
  * **Faithfulness**: “I will not leave you until I have done what I have promised.”

4. Jacob’s Awe and Response (vv.16–19)

* He awakens in awe:   
  * “Surely the LORD is in this place, and I did not know it.”  
* He fears God’s holiness:   
  * “This is none other than the house of God, the gate of heaven.”  
* He sets up the stone as a pillar and names the place **Bethel** (“house of God”).

5. Jacob’s Vow (vv.20–22)

* Jacob makes a vow:   
  * If God will be with him, provide for him, and bring him home in peace, “then the LORD shall be my God.”  
* He pledges that Bethel will be God’s house and that he will give a tenth back to God.  
  * Shows faith in progress: Jacob is still learning trust.   
  * He is still learning what it means to trust fully.

6. Theological Significance

* **Covenant Continuity**:   
  * God reaffirms Abraham’s covenant promises to Jacob, despite Jacob’s failures  
    * God’s purposes are advanced by grace.  
    * Covenant blessing advances by God’s grace, even through flawed covenant partners.  
  * Key covenant promise: “I am with you.”  
* **God’s Presence**:   
  * The dream emphasizes God’s nearness and ongoing connection with His people, bridging heaven and earth.  
  * The presence of God sustains His people as His plan unfolds.  
* **Christological Fulfillment**:   
  * Jesus alludes to this vision in John 1:51, presenting Himself as the true “stairway” where heaven and earth meet.  
  * In Christ, God’s covenant presence is fully realized.  
* **Faith Journey**:   
  * Jacob’s journey mirrors Israel’s story — wandering yet upheld by God’s promises.  
  * Jacob’s vow shows faith in progress — God patiently works with imperfect covenant partners.  
  * Points forward to the pilgrim life of God’s people awaiting the final “Bethel”   
    * God’s dwelling with humanity (Revelation 21).  
* **Worship and Memorial**:   
  * Bethel becomes a sacred place, marking God’s initiative in revealing Himself.

##### Discussion Questions:

* Why is it significant that God meets Jacob while he is fleeing and vulnerable?  
* What does the vision of the ladder teach us about God’s presence and accessibility?  
* How do the promises given to Jacob connect back to Abraham and forward to Christ?  
* Jacob’s vow shows “faith in progress.” How does God’s patience with Jacob encourage us in our own faith journey?  
* How can this story shape the way we think about God’s presence in our daily lives?

##### Gospel Connection

* Jesus is the true Bethel — “God with us” (Emmanuel), the one who bridges heaven and earth.  
* In Christ, the covenant promises (land, people, blessing) are fulfilled and extended to all nations.  
* Our assurance of God’s presence is secured in Him (Matthew 28:20).

#### Reflection & Application

**Personal Reflection Prompts:**

* Where is God calling you to trust Him in an unfamiliar or uncertain situation?  
* Identify one area of your life where you feel alone or uncertain, and this week intentionally rest in God’s promise: “I am with you.”  
* What promises from God feel delayed in your life — and how are you responding?  
* How does Abraham’s story encourage you to persevere in faith?

**Group Prayer Ideas:**

* Thank God for His faithfulness across generations.  
* Pray for trust when God's promises feel delayed or unclear.  
* Thank God for His covenant faithfulness, confess ways we struggle to trust His presence, and ask for eyes to see His nearness in daily life.  
* Ask God to make your group a blessing to others, just as He called Abraham to bless the nations.

#### Suggested Follow-Up or Homework

* **Memory Verse:** Genesis 15:6 — *“And he believed the Lord, and he counted it to him as righteousness.”*  
* **Reflection Exercise:**  
   Write down 1–2 areas where you feel “in-between” like Abraham — waiting for something God has promised. Journal a prayer of trust to God.

### Lesson 2.2 – Blessing to the Nations

#### Key Texts:

* **Genesis 17:1–14** — God establishes the covenant sign of circumcision  
* **Galatians 3:6–9, 26–29** — Believers in Christ as Abraham’s true offspring

**Theme:** The covenant with Abraham includes a call to faith, a sign of belonging, and a promise of blessing for all nations through Christ.

#### Part 1: The Covenant Sign of Circumcision (Genesis 17:1–14)

**Read:** Genesis 17:1–14

One person reads vv.1–8 (God’s promises and Abraham’s new name), another reads vv.9–14 (covenant obligations and sign).

##### Key Concepts:

1. God’s Self-Revelation (v.1)

* God appears as **El Shaddai** (“God Almighty”).  
* Abram is called to “walk before me, and be blameless.”  
* Emphasizes covenant relationship  
  * God is sovereign; Abram must live faithfully.

2. Covenant Confirmation (vv.2–3)

* God promises to confirm His covenant with Abram and multiply him exceedingly.  
* Abram falls on his face   
  * A posture of reverence and submission.

3. Abram’s New Name and Role (vv.4–6)

* Abram (“exalted father”) becomes **Abraham** (“father of a multitude”).  
* Promise expanded:   
  * Abraham will be the father of many nations, not just one.  
* Kings will come from his line   
  * Pointing forward to Israel’s monarchy and ultimately Christ.

4. Everlasting Covenant (vv.7–8)

* God promises to be God to Abraham and his offspring forever   
  * The heart of covenant relationship.  
* The covenant is described as everlasting.  
* Land of Canaan is given as an everlasting possession.

5. Covenant Sign: Circumcision (vv.9–11)

* Every male descendant must be circumcised.  
* Circumcision is a sign of the covenant   
  * An outward mark of belonging to God’s people.  
  * It signifies consecration, covenant identity, and continuity.

6. Covenant Obligation and Warning (vv.12–14)

* Circumcision applies to every male   
  * Household members, slaves, and foreigners alike.  
* It is to be performed on the eighth day.  
* Anyone who is not circumcised will be **“cut off”** from the people — breaking covenant.

7. Theological Significance

* **Covenant Identity:**   
  * Circumcision marks out God’s covenant people.  
  * God’s people are marked off from the world.  
* **Faith and Obedience:**   
  * Faith (Genesis 15:6) is the covenant basis; obedience (circumcision) is the covenant sign.  
  * Abraham was justified by faith (Gen 15:6). Circumcision came later (Gen 17), showing that signs flow from faith, not replace it.  
* **Continuity of Covenant:**   
  * This develops and expands the promises first given in Genesis 12 and 15.  
* **Christological Fulfillment:**   
  * In the new covenant, circumcision is fulfilled in Christ and applied spiritually through the circumcision of the heart by the Spirit (Romans 2:28–29; Colossians 2:11–12).  
  * Baptism functions as the new covenant sign of belonging to God’s people.  
* **Everlasting Covenant:**   
  * God’s commitment to Abraham’s descendants anticipates the eternal covenant fulfilled in Christ.  
  * This anticipates the “I will be their God, and they shall be my people” of the new covenant (Jer 31:33).

##### Discussion Questions:

* Why do you think God gives a physical sign for a spiritual promise?  
* How would circumcision mark Abraham’s descendants as different from the nations around them?  
* What does this teach us about the importance of visible commitment in our faith?  
* Why does God change Abram’s name to Abraham? What does this teach us about covenant identity?  
* Why is it significant that Abraham was declared righteous by faith (Gen 15) before receiving the covenant sign (Gen 17)?  
* What does circumcision teach us about the seriousness of belonging to God’s people?  
* How is God’s “everlasting covenant” promise in v.7 fulfilled in Christ?  
* How should the covenant sign (then circumcision, now baptism) shape how we think about our identity and mission?

##### Gospel Connection:

* Just as circumcision marked Abraham’s descendants as God’s covenant people, baptism now marks those in Christ as members of the new covenant community.  
* Our righteousness, like Abraham’s, is by **faith alone**, not by outward rituals (Romans 4).  
* In Christ, the everlasting covenant promise — “I will be your God” — finds its ultimate fulfillment.

#### Part 2: Abraham’s Seed and Global Blessing (Galatians 3:6–9, 26–29)

**Read:** Galatians 3:6–9, 26–29

##### Key Concepts:

1. Abraham’s Faith as the Model (vv.6–7)

* Paul quotes Genesis 15:6: *“Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness.”*  
* Righteousness comes by faith, not by works of the law.  
* Those who share Abraham’s faith are his true children  
  * Covenant membership is defined by faith, not ethnicity.  
  * True children of Abraham are those who believe, not merely those descended by blood.

  2. Blessing of Abraham for the Nations (vv.8–9)

* Scripture foresaw that God would justify the Gentiles by faith.  
* The promise to Abraham (“All nations will be blessed through you,” Gen 12:3) anticipated the gospel.  
* Believers — Jew or Gentile — share in Abraham’s blessing through faith in Christ.  
  3. Sons of God through Faith in Christ (v.26)  
* All who believe in Christ are **“sons of God”**   
  * All believers in Christ are adopted as God’s children — full covenant heirs.  
* Adoption into God’s family is based on faith, not heritage or law.  
  4. Baptism and Union with Christ (v.27)  
* Baptism symbolizes union with Christ:   
  * Believers have “put on Christ.”  
* This imagery conveys new identity and belonging in the covenant community.  
  * Covenant identity now rests in Christ, not in ethnicity, gender, or social status.

  5. Equality in Christ (v.28)

* In Christ, old divisions are abolished: *“neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female.”*  
  * In Christ, distinctions do not determine covenant standing.  
* This does not erase distinctions of role or identity but means all are equally covenant heirs in Christ.  
  6. Heirs of the Promise (v.29)  
* Believers in Christ are Abraham’s offspring and heirs according to the promise.  
* The Abrahamic covenant’s promises of blessing and inheritance culminate in Christ and are shared by all who belong to Him.  
  7. Theological Significance  
* **Justification by Faith:**   
  * Abrahamic covenant anticipated global blessing   
    * Paul shows this fulfilled in Christ.  
  * Rooted in Abraham’s story, fulfilled in Christ, offered to all.  
* **Covenant Inclusion:**   
  * Membership in God’s covenant people is determined by faith, not ethnicity, circumcision, or law-keeping.  
* **Unity in Christ:**   
  * The new covenant community transcends social, cultural, and gender divisions.  
  * God’s covenant family is no longer defined by circumcision or Torah-keeping but by union with Christ.  
* **Fulfillment of the Abrahamic Covenant:**   
  * God’s promise to bless the nations through Abraham finds its climax in the gospel of Jesus Christ.  
* **Global Mission:**  
  * The Abrahamic promise of blessing to all nations drives the mission of the church  
    * Bringing the gospel to Jew and Gentile alike.

* What does it mean that Abraham was justified “before” circumcision?  
* How does this affect how we think about salvation and inclusion in God’s family?  
* How does Galatians 3 expand the Abrahamic promise beyond Israel?

##### Gospel Connection

* Just as Abraham was justified by faith in God’s promise, so we are justified by faith in Jesus Christ, the promised offspring.  
* In Him, we inherit the blessings of Abraham: justification, adoption, and the Spirit.  
* The church is the covenant family of God — united in Christ and sent to bless the nations.

### Lesson 2.3 – Sojourning in the Promised Land

#### Key Texts:

* **Hebrews 11:8–16** — Faith that looks forward to a better country  
* **Genesis 50:22–26** — Joseph's death and hope for God’s future deliverance

**Theme:** God’s covenant people live as pilgrims in a land of promise, trusting His faithfulness even when the promises are not fully seen.

#### Part 1: Faith on the Move (Hebrews 11:8–16)

**Read:** Hebrews 11:8–16

Have one person read vv.8–10 (Abraham’s obedience), another vv.11–12 (Sarah’s faith), and another vv.13–16 (pilgrim identity and hope).

##### Key Concepts:

1. Abraham’s Obedient Faith (vv.8–10)

* **Obedience to God’s call**:   
  * Abraham left his homeland without knowing where he was going—faith means trusting God’s word even without full details.  
* **Sojourning in the land**:   
  * Abraham lived in tents, as did Isaac and Jacob, heirs of the same promise—indicating the **already/not yet** tension of the covenant promises.  
  * Lived in tents in Canaan, pointing to temporary, incomplete fulfillment.  
* **Looking for a city**:   
  * Abraham’s hope pointed beyond Canaan to a permanent, heavenly city designed and built by God—faith lifts the eyes beyond earthly fulfillment.

2. Sarah’s Faith and God’s Power (v.11–12)

* Though barren and past childbearing age, Sarah received power to conceive because she trusted God’s promise.  
* From one man “as good as dead” came descendants as numerous as the stars—God brings life out of death, fulfilling His covenant promises.

3. The Pilgrim Perspective (vv.13–14)

* These patriarchs all died in faith, not receiving the promises in full, but seeing them from afar.  
* They confessed themselves to be “strangers and exiles” on the earth—faith involves acknowledging that our true homeland is still future.

4. Seeking a Better Country (vv.15–16)

* If they had longed for their old homeland, they could have returned. Instead, they desired a better country, a heavenly one.  
* God is not ashamed to be called their God, because He has prepared for them a city—the ultimate fulfillment of the covenant promises in God’s presence.

5. Theological Significance

* **Faith and Obedience**:   
  * Abraham’s journey shows that faith is active trust, moving forward even without knowing the full plan.  
  * Abraham models covenant response: trust, obedience, and patient waiting.  
  *  Faith doesn’t mean having everything in hand — it means trusting God's promise while still waiting.   
    * Abraham’s journey is a prototype of Christian life: walking by faith, not sight (cf. 2 Corinthians 5:7).  
* **Already/Not Yet Covenant Tension**:   
  * The patriarchs experienced partial fulfillment (land, descendants) but looked ahead to ultimate fulfillment.  
  * This highlights covenant continuity: God’s promises unfold progressively through history.  
* **Pilgrim Identity**:   
  * Believers are exiles in the present world, awaiting the true homeland God has promised.  
* **Heavenly City**:   
  * God’s covenant purposes culminate in the eternal dwelling of God with His people—a theme picked up in Revelation 21–22.  
* **Covenant Continuity**:   
  * The promises given to Abraham point beyond earthly Canaan to the eternal inheritance secured in Christ.

##### Discussion Questions:

* What does Abraham’s obedience in leaving home teach us about the nature of faith?  
* How does Sarah’s story encourage us when God’s promises seem impossible?  
* What does it mean for us today to live as “strangers and exiles”?  
* How do these verses connect the Abrahamic covenant to our hope in Christ?  
* What does it mean that God has prepared a “better country” for us? How does this shape daily life?

##### Gospel Connection

* Abraham looked forward to a city with foundations—fulfilled in Christ, who brings us into the heavenly Jerusalem (Hebrews 12:22; Revelation 21).  
* Just as Abraham’s righteousness came by faith, ours does too—through trusting in Jesus, the promised offspring (Galatians 3:16).  
* Christ secures the “better country” where God will dwell with His people forever.

#### Part 2: Faith in Death (Genesis 50:22–26)

**Read Together:** Genesis 50:22–26

Have one person read vv.22–23 (Joseph’s life and family legacy), another vv.24–26 (Joseph’s final words and burial instructions).

##### Key Concepts:

1. Joseph’s Long Life (v.22)

* Joseph remains in Egypt with his family and lives to 110 years—a full, blessed lifespan by ancient standards.  
* His life reflects God’s covenant blessing and preservation, despite being outside the Promised Land.  
  * Egypt, though prosperous, was not his ultimate home.

2. Covenant Continuity Through Generations (v.23)

* Joseph sees his great-grandchildren, including those of Ephraim and Manasseh.  
* The emphasis on multiple generations underscores the covenant promise of descendants (Genesis 12:2; 15:5).

3. Joseph’s Dying Declaration of Faith (vv.24–25)

* Joseph reminds his brothers: **“God will surely come to your aid”** (repeated twice for emphasis).  
* He looks back to God’s covenant promises to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and forward to their fulfillment in the Exodus.  
* Joseph makes Israel swear to carry his bones up from Egypt.  
  * Trust in God’s future action and aligns him with the Promised Land, not Egypt.

4. Joseph’s Death and Burial in Egypt (v.26)

* Though Joseph dies in Egypt, he does not see it as his final home.  
* His request anticipates the Exodus (fulfilled in Exodus 13:19; Joshua 24:32).  
* His burial instructions embody covenant hope—trusting that God’s promises extend beyond his lifetime.

5. Theological Significance

* **Faith in God’s Covenant Promises**:   
  * Joseph anchors his final words in the certainty that God will act according to His word.  
  * He lives by God’s word even when the promise isn’t yet fulfilled.  
* **Pilgrim Identity**:   
  * Even at the height of Egyptian success, Joseph identifies with God’s covenant people and the Promised Land, not Egypt.  
* **Hope Beyond Death**:   
  * Joseph’s faith points beyond his own lifetime, modeling trust in God’s redemptive plan.  
* **Bridge to Exodus**:   
  * The passage closes Genesis not with finality but with anticipation—God’s covenant promises are still unfolding.  
  * This sets the stage for God’s covenant with Moses and the people of Israel at Sinai.

##### Discussion Questions:

* Why do you think Joseph cared so much about where he was buried?  
* What does this say about his belief in God’s covenant promises?  
* How can we cultivate the kind of hope that carries through suffering and even death?  
* What does Joseph’s faith teach us about trusting God when we don’t see immediate fulfillment?  
* How does Joseph’s identification with the Promised Land shape our view of identity and belonging?  
* Why does it matter that Joseph emphasized “God will surely come to your aid”?  
* In what ways can we pass down covenant faith to future generations like Joseph did?  
* How does this passage prepare us for what’s coming in Exodus?

##### Gospel Connection

* Joseph trusted in God’s future redemption. Jesus is that Redeemer—fulfilling the hope of all God’s covenant promises.  
* Just as Joseph asked to be taken to the Promised Land, believers long for the eternal Promised Land—the new creation secured by Christ.  
* In Christ, God has come to our aid—rescuing us from sin and death, and leading us to glory.

#### Reflection & Application

**Personal Reflection Prompts:**

* Where in your life are you living in “tent-like” faith — trusting without seeing?  
* How does your hope in God shape your daily choices?  
* What is one area where you need to renew your trust in God’s promises?

**Group Prayer Ideas:**

* Thank God for His promises that endure beyond this life.  
* Pray for faith to endure in seasons of uncertainty or waiting.  
* Ask God to make your group a people of hope, grounded in His future.

#### Key Theological Themes

* **Sojourning Faith** — Believers live as “resident aliens,” rooted in the hope of God’s eternal kingdom (Philippians 3:20).  
* **Forward-Looking Covenant** — God's covenant promises stretch beyond generations and even lifetimes.  
* **Death in Hope** — Faith is not just about how we live but also **how we die** — in full assurance of God’s future.

### Lesson 3.1 – Moses and the Exodus Road

#### Key Texts:

* **Exodus 1–14 (overview)** — Israel’s slavery and deliverance  
* **Exodus 12:1–30 (focus)** — The Passover and final plague

**Theme:** *God delivers His people from slavery in Egypt, establishing them as a redeemed community through whom His purposes will be revealed.*

#### Part 1: Slavery and the Cry for Deliverance (Exodus 1–6 Summary)

**Reading:** Exodus 1:8–14; 2:23–25; 3:1–12

Assign three readers — one for the oppression (1:8–14), one for the cry and God’s remembrance (2:23–25), and one for the burning bush and call (3:1–12).

##### Key Concepts:

1. Israel’s Oppression in Egypt (Exodus 1:8–14)

a. A New Pharaoh (v.8)

* A king arises “who did not know Joseph,” symbolizing a loss of covenant favor and the start of persecution.  
* Reflects a shift in political power and a new posture toward Israel: fear and control.

b. Fear of Israel’s Growth (vv.9–10)

* The Egyptians fear the increasing population of the Israelites.  
* Echoes the blessing of fruitfulness from the Abrahamic covenant (Gen 12:2; 17:6), now seen as a threat by Pharaoh.

c. Oppression and Slavery (vv.11–14)

* Pharaoh subjects Israel to forced labor and affliction.  
* Harsh treatment is intended to suppress, but the people continue to multiply.  
* This tension reveals that God’s covenant promises are alive, even amid suffering.

2. God’s Compassionate Response (Exodus 2:23–25)

a. Israel Cries Out (v.23)

* After years of oppression and the death of Pharaoh, the people groan under slavery and cry out for help.  
* This is not just generic suffering—it's a covenantal lament.

b. God Hears, Sees, and Knows (vv.24–25)

* Key verbs: “God heard… remembered… saw… knew.”  
* “Remembered His covenant” doesn’t imply He forgot, but that He is now ready to act on it.  
* God’s response is rooted in His faithfulness to the covenant made with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

3. God’s Call to Moses (Exodus 3:1–12)

a. The Burning Bush (vv.1–3)

* God appears to Moses in the wilderness in a bush that burns but is not consumed—a sign of His holy presence and a symbol of hope: afflicted, but not destroyed.

b. God’s Self-Disclosure and Mission (vv.4–6)

* God calls Moses by name and reveals Himself as “the God of your father, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.”  
  * This links the present to the patriarchs.  
* God sees, hears, and comes down to deliver and bring Israel into the land.  
* This directly connects the mission ahead to the covenantal identity of God.

c. God’s Compassion and Intent to Deliver (vv.7–9)

* God sees the affliction, hears the cry, knows the suffering—and is now coming down to deliver and bring His people into the land He promised.  
* This fulfills the Abrahamic promise of land (Gen 15:13–14).

d. Moses’s Commission (vv.10–12)

* God sends Moses to Pharaoh to bring His people out of Egypt.  
* Moses objects, but God responds: “I will be with you”  
  * Divine presence is the guarantee of success.  
* The sign: worship on this mountain (Sinai), looking ahead to covenant renewal at Sinai (Exodus 19–24).

Theological Significance

* **Covenant Faithfulness**:   
  * God’s action is driven not by Israel’s merit but by His covenant commitment to Abraham’s family.  
* **Suffering Within God’s Plan**:   
  * Israel’s oppression doesn’t negate the promise—it becomes the stage for redemption.  
  *  God’s covenants don’t prevent suffering, but they guarantee that God will not abandon His promises.  
  * The affliction of Israel is not a detour—it’s the stage for God’s redeeming work.  
* **Divine Compassion and Initiative**:   
  * God sees, hears, remembers, and moves. He is not distant but deeply involved.  
  * Genesis ends in anticipation (Joseph’s bones); Exodus begins with God initiating fulfillment.  
* **Covenant-Mission Connection**:   
  * God’s covenant with the patriarchs now issues a call and mission  
    * deliverance leading to worship and service, not just freedom (Exodus 3:12).  
  * God’s covenant people are not just saved *from* something (Egypt), but *for* something—relationship with God.  
* **God’s Presence as the Anchor**:   
  * The promise “I will be with you” anticipates the tabernacle, the law, and ultimately Immanuel—God with us.

##### Discussion Questions:

* Why do you think God waited so long to respond to Israel’s oppression?  
* How do God’s four actions (heard, remembered, saw, knew) comfort you in seasons of suffering?  
* What does this tell us about God's character and involvement?  
* What strikes you about God’s response to Israel’s groaning in 2:23–25?  
* How does this passage show that God’s silence doesn’t mean absence?  
* Why do you think God introduces Himself to Moses as “the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob”?  
* How does the call of Moses prepare us for understanding Jesus' role in the new covenant?  
* What does it mean that God promises to be “with” Moses? How does that give us confidence in our own calling?

##### Gospel Connection

* Moses is a type of Christ—a chosen mediator through whom God will rescue His people.  
* The “I have come down to deliver” of Exodus 3 anticipates the Incarnation, where God truly comes down in Jesus.  
* Just as Israel’s redemption led to worship at Sinai, so our redemption in Christ leads to worship and covenant fellowship in the Spirit.

#### Part 2: The Power of Redemption and the Passover (Exodus 12:1–30)

**Read:** Exodus 12:1–30

Divide the reading among three voices:

* vv.1–20 (God’s instructions),  
* vv.21–28 (Moses and the people’s obedience),  
* vv.29–30 (God’s final act of judgment).

##### Key Concepts:

1. God Institutes a New Beginning (vv.1–2)

* God reorients Israel’s calendar around the Passover:   
  * “This month shall be the beginning of months for you.”  
* Redemption becomes the marker of time—a new identity rooted in God’s saving work.  
* This signifies that the people are being redefined not by Egypt but by God’s act of deliverance.

2. The Passover Instructions (vv.3–11)

* Every household is to select a spotless lamb, to be sacrificed and eaten.  
* The lamb’s blood is to be put on the doorposts and lintel as a sign.  
* The meal is to be eaten in haste, with belt fastened and staff in hand—ready for departure.  
  * God is about to act.  
* The focus is on obedience, preparedness, and trust in God’s provision.

3. The Meaning of the Blood (vv.12–13)

* God announces the tenth and final plague—the death of every firstborn in Egypt.  
* The blood is a sign:   
  * When God sees the blood, He will “pass over” that house and not bring judgment upon it.  
* Salvation comes not by ethnicity or merit, but by the substitutionary death of the lamb and faith in God’s word.

4. A Perpetual Memorial (vv.14–20)

* The Passover is to be observed throughout generations as a statute forever.  
* Linked to the Feast of Unleavened Bread, a symbol of haste and purity—leaven is removed.  
* This becomes Israel’s foundational salvation event, to be remembered and retold.

5. Obedience and the First Passover (vv.21–28)

* Moses conveys the instructions to the elders of Israel.  
* Emphasis on the slaughter, blood, and staying inside.  
* The people obey, and worship (v.27), recognizing God’s deliverance plan.  
* This act of obedience leads to their preservation.  
  * Deliverance requires not just knowledge but trusting action.

6. The Tenth Plague and Deliverance (vv.29–30)

* At midnight, the Lord strikes down all the firstborn in Egypt—from Pharaoh to the lowest servant.  
  * Judgment and deliverance occur simultaneously—one for Egypt, the other for God’s covenant people.  
* A great cry rises in Egypt, but not a single Israelite dies—those under the blood are spared.  
* This climactic act forces Pharaoh to let Israel go, beginning the Exodus.

7. Theological Significance

* **Redemption Before Atonement:**  
  * God saves before He commands—redemption precedes law.  
  * Exodus 12 is the foundational act of salvation that will ground the covenant at Sinai (Exodus 19–24).  
* **God’s Faithfulness to His Covenant:**  
  * God acts because He “remembered His covenant” (Exodus 2:24).  
  * This is the fulfillment of Genesis 15:13–14  
    * Abraham’s descendants delivered from slavery.  
* **Substitution and Atonement**:   
  * The lamb dies in the place of the firstborn—anticipating the gospel.  
  * A key theological pattern is established:   
    * Deliverance through blood.  
  * This anticipates the new covenant in Christ, our Passover Lamb (1 Corinthians 5:7).  
* **Faith Expressed in Obedience**:   
  * Israel’s deliverance depends on trusting God and obeying His word.  
* **Covenant Identity**:   
  * God marks His people by their response to His word, not their ancestry.  
* **God’s Sovereignty and Judgment**:   
  * God executes justice on Egypt while providing mercy for His people.  
* **Redemptive Memorial**:   
  * The Passover becomes a ritual of remembrance, shaping Israel’s identity as the redeemed people of God.  
* **Gospel Typology**:   
  * The Passover lamb points forward to Jesus Christ, the Lamb of God, whose blood delivers from final judgment (cf. John 1:29; 1 Cor. 5:7).

##### Discussion Questions:

* Why do you think God required Israel to participate in the Passover preparation?  
* How does the idea of substitution (the lamb dying in place of the firstborn) deepen our understanding of Jesus' death?  
* In what ways is remembering central to covenant life?  
* Why do you think God made the Passover the beginning of Israel’s calendar?  
* What does the blood of the lamb represent in this passage, and how is that fulfilled in Jesus?  
* How does Israel’s obedience (vv.21–28) challenge our understanding of faith today?  
* Why was it important for the people to remember the Passover through generations?  
* How does this passage show the relationship between judgment and salvation?

##### Gospel Connection

* Jesus is our true Passover Lamb (John 1:29; 1 Cor. 5:7)—slain so that judgment “passes over” us.  
* Just as Israel was saved not by merit but by the blood and their trust in God’s word, so are we.  
* The Lord’s Supper echoes the Passover meal—a covenant meal of remembrance and identity (Luke 22:19–20).

#### Part 3: A People Redeemed to Belong

**Key Verse:** Exodus 6:7 — *“I will take you to be my people, and I will be your God.”*

##### Key Concepts:

1. Covenant Formula: “I will take you to be my people, and I will be your God”

* This is the classic covenant relationship formula used throughout Scripture.  
* It expresses relational commitment, not just legal agreement.   
  * God is binding Himself personally to Israel.

* It shows the goal of redemption:   
  * Not merely freedom from Egypt, but belonging to God.  
* This same formula reappears at key points in redemptive history:  
  * Leviticus 26:12  
  * Jeremiah 31:33 (new covenant)  
  * Revelation 21:3 (final fulfillment)

2. Divine Initiative and Possession: “I will take you…”

* God initiates the relationship; Israel does not earn or request it.  
* “Take” implies choosing, adopting, and making a claim of ownership.  
* Echoes God’s sovereign choice of Israel going back to Abraham (Gen 12; 17).

3. Experiential Knowledge of God: “You shall know that I am the LORD your God”

* This is not merely intellectual knowledge—it’s relational and experiential.  
  * God saves in order to dwell with His people.  
* Israel will “know” Yahweh through His acts of redemption, not abstract theology.  
* The covenant is personal:   
  * The God who redeems is also the God who dwells with and reveals Himself to His people.

4. Redemption as Covenant Entry: “who has brought you out…”

* God’s redemptive act—deliverance from slavery—is the basis for the covenant relationship.  
* God is not just a deliverer, but a covenantal Lord who rescues for the purpose of relationship and worship (cf. Exodus 19:4–6).  
* This establishes the pattern of salvation → covenant → mission that continues throughout Scripture.

5. Theological Significance

* **Covenant Identity:**   
  * Israel becomes the people of God through God’s choosing and redeeming action.  
* **Purpose of Redemption:**   
  * Liberation is not an end in itself; it’s for the sake of knowing and belonging to God.  
* **Covenant Continuity:**   
  * This formula (“I will be your God”) continues across all covenants, including the new covenant in Christ (cf. Hebrews 8:10; Revelation 21:3).  
* **Relational Theology:**   
  * God’s desire is not just to save His people from something (slavery), but to Himself.

Core Message:  
The Exodus is not just about liberation — it’s about forming a new identity. God doesn’t just free Israel; He calls them into covenant relationship. They are to be His people, living by His word, carrying His presence.

Future Connection:  
 This theme continues in Exodus 19 (next lesson), where God formalizes the relationship at Mount Sinai. But even here, God’s goal in redemption is not just rescue — it’s relationship.

##### Discussion Questions:

* How does this story challenge your view of what it means to belong to God?  
* What does it mean for God to say, “I will take you to be my people”? How personal is that?  
* Why is it important that this relationship begins with God’s action, not ours?  
* How does the phrase “you shall know that I am the LORD” speak to how we relate to God today?  
* How is this verse a turning point in Israel’s story — and ours?  
* In what ways does Jesus fulfill the covenant promise of “I will be your God”?

##### Gospel Connection

* In Christ, God’s covenant promise is fulfilled: we are bought with His blood, adopted into His family, and made His people (Galatians 3:29; Titus 2:14).  
* Jesus is the one who brings us out of bondage to sin, into fellowship with God.  
* The new covenant makes this possible not just for Israel, but for all who believe.

**Personal Reflection Prompts:**

* Do you live as someone *set apart* by God’s saving work?  
* What “Egypt” might God be calling you or someone close to you out of right now?

### Lesson 3.2 – Kingdom of Priests, Holy Nation

#### Key Texts:

* **Exodus 19:1–8** — God offers covenant relationship to Israel  
* **Deuteronomy 7:6–11** — A call to holiness and obedience  
* **1 Peter 2:9–10** — The New Covenant fulfillment of Israel’s calling

**Theme:** God calls His redeemed people to live as a holy community that reflects His glory to the nations.

#### Part 1: Covenant at Sinai — A Nation Set Apart (Exodus 19:1–8)

**Read:** Exodus 19:1–8

Have three readers—one for vv.1–2 (arrival), one for vv.3–6 (God’s message), and one for vv.7–8 (the people’s response).

##### Key Concepts:

1. Covenant Setting and Timing (v.1–2)

* Israel arrives at Mount Sinai three months after leaving Egypt.  
* This mountain is where God first appeared to Moses in the burning bush (Exodus 3), and where He promised, *“you shall worship God on this mountain.”*  
* The scene fulfills that promise and sets the stage for the formal giving of the Mosaic covenant.  
  * This is not a random stop—it is the appointed place of covenant encounter.

2. God’s Covenant Initiative (v.3–4)

* God calls Moses to the mountain to deliver a message to the people.  
* He reminds Israel of what He has already done:  
  * *“I bore you on eagles’ wings”* — poetic imagery of God’s tender, powerful rescue.  
  * *“and brought you to myself”* — redemption leads to relationship, not just rescue or independence.

3. Conditional Covenant Offer (v.5)

> “Now therefore, if you will indeed obey my voice and keep my covenant…”

* Introduces the conditional nature of the Sinai covenant:   
  * Obedience leads to blessing and unique identity.  
* This doesn’t negate grace—obedience is a response to redemption, not a prerequisite for it.

4. Covenant Identity and Purpose (v.5–6)

If Israel obeys, they will be:

a. God’s Treasured Possession

* *Hebrew: segullah*   
* A royal term meaning special, chosen property.  
* God owns all creation, but Israel is set apart for His redemptive purposes.

b. A Kingdom of Priests

* Israel is to mediate God’s presence to the nations.  
* The whole nation is called to represent God through worship, holiness, and witness.  
* This anticipates the New Testament idea of the priesthood of all believers (1 Peter 2:9).

c. A Holy Nation

* “Holy” means set apart — morally, spiritually, and missionally.  
* Israel’s obedience will display God’s character to the world.

5. The People’s Response (v.7–8)

* Moses presents God’s words to the elders and people.  
* The people respond: *“All that the LORD has spoken we will do.”*  
* Their eager affirmation of covenant obedience sets the stage for the formal giving of the Law (Exodus 20+).  
* Their response is right, though they don’t yet grasp the depth of what obedience will require.

6. Theological Significance

* **Covenant Fulfillment:**   
  * God is bringing to fruition His promises to Abraham—offspring, land (on the way), and relationship.  
* **Grace Before Law:**   
  * God rescues first (*“I brought you to myself”*), then calls for obedience.  
  * Exodus 19 is not a legal burden, but a relational invitation.  
* **Covenant as Vocation:**   
  * Israel’s calling is not just to be blessed, but to be a blessing—a priestly people for the nations.  
  * The threefold identity (v.6) reflects God’s missional purpose:   
    * A people who worship, reflect, and represent Him.  
  * This anticipates the church’s identity as a royal priesthood (1 Peter 2:9)—the new covenant extension of Israel’s calling.  
* **Covenant Relationship:**   
  * This is the moment when God formally proposes a covenant marriage to Israel (see Hosea and Jeremiah 2 for later prophetic reflection).  
    * This is a covenant marriage proposal—God chooses Israel as His own.  
  * This moment prepares the way for the giving of the Ten Commandments and covenant law.  
    * Law flows from relationship—not to earn salvation, but to reflect God’s holiness.

* What does it mean to be God’s “treasured possession”?  
* How does the idea of a “kingdom of priests” expand your understanding of Israel’s mission?  
* What is the connection between grace (v.4) and obedience (v.5)?  
* Why is it important that God reminds Israel what He has already done before asking for obedience?  
* What does it mean to be a “kingdom of priests” today as the church (1 Peter 2:9)?  
* How can we guard against seeing obedience as legalism rather than a response to covenant love?  
* Why do you think Israel says “All that the LORD has spoken we will do”? How does that reflect (or challenge) your own response to God?  
* How does this passage help us understand God’s desire not just to save, but to dwell with His people?

##### Gospel Connection

* Jesus fulfills this covenant call:   
  * He is the true holy One, the perfect mediator, and through Him we become God’s treasured people (Titus 2:14).  
* Through Christ, the church becomes a kingdom of priests, declaring the excellencies of Him who called us (1 Peter 2:9).  
* Obedience still matters—but it flows from the grace of the gospel, not as a means to earn it.

#### Part 2: Holiness, Obedience, and Covenant Faithfulness (Deuteronomy 7:6–11)

**Read:** Deuteronomy 7:6–11

One person reads vv.6–8 (God’s choice and love), another vv.9–11 (God’s faithfulness and call to obedience).

##### Key Concepts:

1. Israel’s Covenant Identity (v.6)

> *“For you are a people holy to the LORD your God. The LORD your God has chosen you to be a people for His treasured possession…”*

* **Holy people**:   
  * Israel is set apart for God’s purposes—morally, spiritually, and missionally.  
* **Chosen by God**:   
  * Their identity is rooted in God’s gracious election, not their merit.  
* **Treasured possession (segullah)**:   
  * Covenant language—God values and protects Israel as uniquely His among all nations (cf. Exodus 19:5–6).

2. God’s Gracious Choosing (vv.7–8)

> *“It was not because you were more in number… but it is because the LORD loves you and is keeping the oath…”*

* **Not chosen for greatness**:   
  * Israel’s election is not based on size, power, or virtue.  
* **Because the LORD loves you**:   
  * Their selection is rooted in God’s sovereign love—self-originating and self-sustaining.  
* **Oath to the fathers**:   
  * God is keeping His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (Genesis 12, 15, 17). His love is relational and historical.

3. God’s Character: Faithful Deliverer (v.8b)

> *“…the LORD brought you out with a mighty hand and redeemed you from the house of slavery…”*

* God's covenant love is proven in action—specifically in the Exodus redemption.  
* "Mighty hand" and "redeemed" are classic Exodus-salvation language.  
* God is both Redeemer and Promise-Keeper.

4. God’s Covenant Faithfulness and Justice (v.9–10)

> *“Know therefore that the LORD your God is God, the faithful God who keeps covenant and steadfast love…”*

* **Theological anchor**:   
  * God is faithful and keeps His covenant love (hesed) for those who love Him and keep His commandments.  
  * God’s love is loyal and committed across generations.  
* **Justice**:   
  * God will also repay those who hate Him—He is not only merciful, but just.  
  * Moral accountability is built into the covenant—faith and obedience go together.  
  * Covenant relationship includes both intimacy and accountability.

5. Call to Obedience (v.11)

> *“You shall therefore be careful to do the commandment…”*

* Obedience is the appropriate response to God’s love, grace, and faithfulness.  
* Israel's call is not to earn God's favor, but to live out their identity as His people.  
* This is covenantal obedience, not legalistic obligation.

Theological Significance

* **Election by Grace**:   
  * Israel was chosen purely by God’s love, not merit—this anticipates grace-based salvation.  
  * Deuteronomy 7 reinforces that Israel’s election is undeserved, sustained by God’s love.  
* **Covenant Continuity**:   
  * God acts in Deuteronomy because of promises made in Genesis—He is consistent across the storyline.  
* **Faithful Love**:   
  * God's hesed is at the heart of covenant—loyal, enduring, and generational.  
* **Obedience as Response**:   
  * As a holy people, Israel is set apart not only for relationship but for mission—to display God’s character to the nations.  
  * This anticipates the new covenant church’s calling as a kingdom of priests (1 Peter 2:9).  
  * Obedience is rooted in gratitude, not in earning covenant membership.  
    * Obedience is always a covenant response to grace—never a means of earning.  
    * Deuteronomy 7:11 frames obedience as careful, loving faithfulness to a God who loved first.  
* **Echo in the New Covenant**:   
  * Believers in Christ are also called God's chosen, holy, and treasured (1 Peter 2:9), by grace through faith.

* Why is it important that God chose Israel based on His love, not their merit?  
* How does covenant loyalty (God’s and ours) shape daily life?  
* In what ways are you tempted to find your identity in something other than being God’s “set-apart” person?  
* Why does God emphasize that Israel was not chosen because of their greatness?  
* How does understanding God’s grace help us approach obedience differently?  
* What does it mean to be God’s “treasured possession”?  
* How does this passage challenge a performance-based view of God’s love?  
* How might this covenant identity shape your sense of purpose and calling today?

##### Gospel Connection

* The same God who chose Israel has, in Christ, chosen believers to be His people—not by merit, but by grace through faith (Ephesians 1:4–5; Titus 3:5).  
* The church now bears the same identity: holy, chosen, treasured—called to obey and reflect God’s glory (1 Peter 2:9–10).  
* Jesus fulfills the covenant by living in perfect obedience and dying in our place, securing God’s steadfast love for all who believe.

#### Part 3: Covenant Identity Fulfilled in Christ (1 Peter 2:9–10)

**Read:** 1 Peter 2:9–10

Encourage group members to listen for echoes of earlier covenant passages (e.g., Exodus 19, Deut 7, Hosea 2).

##### Key Concepts:

1. Covenant Identity in Christ (v.9)

> *“But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession…”*

Peter uses four key identity terms, all drawn from Exodus 19:5–6 and Isaiah 43, to describe the church:

a. Chosen Race

* Reflects God’s sovereign initiative—believers are chosen by grace (cf. Deut 7:6–8).  
* Not based on ethnicity or merit, but on union with Christ.  
* Echoes Israel’s calling but now applies to both Jews and Gentiles who believe.

b. Royal Priesthood

* Believers have direct access to God and represent Him to the world.  
* A “kingdom of priests” who both worship God and serve others (cf. Rev 1:6).

c. Holy Nation

* Set apart for God’s purposes—morally and missionally.  
* The church is a new covenant people, defined not by geography or ethnicity, but by holiness and faithfulness.

d. People for His Own Possession (*Greek: laos eis peripoiēsin*)

* Echoes *“treasured possession”* from Exodus 19:5.  
* Believers belong to God in a covenant relationship, formed through Christ’s redemptive work (Titus 2:14).

2. Missional Purpose (v.9b)

> *“…that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light.”*

* **Purpose clause**:   
  * Our identity leads to a mission—to proclaim God’s glory.  
    * “Proclaim” means to announce, declare, bear witness to God’s saving power.  
* “Called out of darkness” recalls the Exodus, but now refers to spiritual rescue through Christ (cf. Colossians 1:13).  
* “Marvelous light” implies transformation, freedom, and revelation.

3. Transformation Through Mercy (v.10)

> *“Once you were not a people, but now you are God’s people; once you had not received mercy, but now you have received mercy.”*

* Direct reference to Hosea 1–2, where God promises to restore a people once rejected.  
* Peter shows this is now fulfilled in the church: once outsiders, now fully included.  
* Covenant inclusion is by mercy, not lineage—a key theme in the New Testament (Romans 9:24–26).  
* Underscores the radical grace of God, making a new people from those who had no place.

Theological Significance

* **New Covenant Fulfillment**:   
  * The church inherits the covenant roles given to Israel—chosen, priestly, holy, treasured—now centered on Christ.  
  * The church doesn’t replace Israel but inherits and fulfills Israel’s covenant role through union with Christ, the true Israelite (Gal 3:16, 29).  
* **Grace-Based Identity**:   
  * Believers are who they are not by effort, but because of God’s call and mercy.  
    * This grace fuels both obedience and mission.  
* **Continuity and Expansion**:   
  * Peter connects Old Testament covenant purposes to their full expression in the church, showing how God’s plan for a people of His own continues across covenants.  
* **Worship and Witness**:   
  * The identity of God’s people is always tied to declaring His excellencies—worship fuels mission.  
  * The church is to **display God’s glory** to the world—not just through words, but through transformed lives.

##### Discussion Questions:

* How does Peter’s description of the church mirror Exodus 19?  
* What does it mean to be a “royal priesthood” in everyday life?  
* How is your life reflecting God's mercy and distinctiveness to the world?  
* How do the titles in verse 9 shape your understanding of what it means to be part of the church?  
* Why is it important that our identity as God’s people is based on mercy, not merit?  
* What does it look like practically to “proclaim His excellencies” in daily life?  
* How does this passage tie together the covenant story from Genesis to Jesus?  
* How can we live more fully as “a people for His own possession” in a world that pressures us to belong elsewhere?

##### Gospel Connection

* Jesus is the true Israel, the faithful priest, the holy Son, and the chosen One.  
* By union with Him, we receive His identity—a new creation people, brought from darkness into light.  
* This covenant is secured not by law or sacrifice, but by Christ’s blood, fulfilling the covenant promises and giving us eternal access to God.

* How do you live out your identity as one of God’s “set-apart” people?  
* What’s one way your group can serve as a “priestly people” to your community this week?

* Ask for renewed clarity and courage to live as a “holy nation” in your context.  
* Pray for the church (globally and locally) to embody this identity in action.

### Lesson 3.3 – Mount Sinai and the Torah

#### Key Texts:

* **Exodus 20:1–21** — The Ten Commandments  
* **Deuteronomy 30:11–20** — The call to choose life through obedience

**Theme:** The giving of the law at Sinai reveals God’s holiness, sets Israel apart, and prepares the way for Christ.

#### Part 1: The Law Reveals God’s Character (Exodus 20:1–21)

**Read**: Exodus 20:1–21 (focus on vv.1–17)

vv.1–2: God’s introduction

vv.3–17: The Ten Commandments

vv.18–21: Israel’s response

##### Key Concepts:

1. Covenant Context (v.1)

> *“And God spoke all these words…”*

* These are not abstract laws—they are “words” from a relational, covenant-making God.  
* This is a continuation of the covenant process initiated in Exodus 19.  
* God Himself speaks directly to the people—underscoring the intimacy and authority of the covenant.

2. Grace Before Law (v.2)

> *“I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery.”*

* God reminds Israel of His redemptive act before issuing commands.  
  * God starts with identity and relationship, not obligation.  
* The law is not a condition of salvation, but a response to salvation.  
  * The commandments are rooted in redemption—Israel obeys because they’ve been freed.  
* Obedience is covenantal—a loving response to God's saving grace.

3. The Ten Commandments (vv.3–17)

These are ten covenant words (Heb. *devarim*) meant to shape Israel’s life as God’s holy nation. They fall into two main categories:

a. Loving God (Commands 1–4)

* **1. No other gods**:  
  * Exclusive loyalty to Yahweh  
* **2. No idols**:   
  * Worship God on His terms, not by images  
* **3. Do not misuse God’s name**:   
  * Reverence in speech and attitude  
* **4. Remember the Sabbath**:   
  * Rest in God’s rhythm and trust in His provision

b. Loving Others (Commands 5–10)

* **5. Honor father and mother**:   
  * Respect for God-given authority  
* **6. Do not murder**:   
  * Protect life  
* **7. Do not commit adultery**:   
  * Honor marriage and covenant faithfulness  
* **8. Do not steal**:   
  * Respect others’ property  
* **9. Do not bear false witness**:   
  * Uphold truth and justice  
* **10. Do not covet**:   
  * Guard the heart from desire that leads to sin

> These commands reflect God’s holy character, and they call Israel to be a distinctive, just, and worshipful community

4. Covenant Fear and Worship (vv.18–21)

* The people tremble at God's presence—thunder, lightning, trumpet sound, and smoke.  
* They are rightly afraid, yet Moses assures them:

   *“Do not fear, for God has come to test you, that the fear of Him may be before you, that you may not sin.”*

* This tension between awe and intimacy shapes true covenant worship.  
  * The fear of the Lord is not to repel, but to shape obedience.  
* Israel stands at a distance, while Moses draws near.  
  * Israel’s fear anticipates the need for a mediator—fulfilled ultimately in Jesus (Hebrews 9:15).

Theological Significance

* **Moral Framework of the Covenant**:   
  * The Ten Commandments are the ethical core of the Mosaic covenant.  
  * Schreiner emphasizes: The law is not a ladder to climb to God, but the way to live in covenant communion with Him.  
* **Holiness and Mission**:   
  * Obedience sets Israel apart as a holy nation and a light to the nations (Deut 4:6–8).  
  * The commandments equip Israel to reflect God’s holiness to the nations (Deut 4:6–8).  
    * The commandments are not simply rules; they are a worshipful response to God’s grace.  
* **God-Centered Life**:   
  * The structure prioritizes God first, then neighbor love—the very structure Jesus affirms (Matthew 22:37–40).  
  * The tenth commandment (“You shall not covet”) reveals that God’s law reaches into the heart.  
    * This sets the stage for the new covenant, where God will write His law on the heart (Jeremiah 31:33).  
* **Law as Grace**:   
  * The law is a gift, not a burden—it teaches Israel how to live as redeemed people.  
* **Need for a Mediator**:   
  * The people’s fear and Moses’s intercession point to the ultimate need for Christ, the true Mediator of the new covenant (Hebrews 8–9).

##### Discussion Questions:

* What does the introduction to the commandments tell us about grace and obedience?  
* Which commandment stands out to you the most — and why?  
* How do these laws reflect God's holiness and justice?  
* Why does God begin the Ten Commandments by reminding Israel of their redemption?  
* Which of these commands feel most relevant or challenging to our cultural moment?  
* How do the first four commandments shape your understanding of worship?  
* What does the people’s fear teach us about God’s holiness and our need for a mediator?  
* How does Jesus fulfill and deepen the meaning of the Ten Commandments?

##### Gospel Connection

* Jesus obeyed every command perfectly, fulfilling the law on our behalf (Matthew 5:17).  
* He is the better Moses, the perfect Mediator, who stands between God and man (Hebrews 8–9).  
* Through Him, we are freed not only from the penalty of sin but also empowered to live righteously by the Spirit (Romans 8:4).

#### Part 2: The Covenant Choice (Deuteronomy 30:11–20)

**Read**: Deuteronomy 30:11–20

##### Key Concepts:

1. God's Word Is Accessible and Doable (vv.11–14)

> *“This commandment that I command you today is not too hard for you, neither is it far off…”*

* **The law is near**:   
  * God's will is not mysterious or out of reach.  
* **No excuse**:   
  * It’s not in heaven or across the sea—God has clearly revealed His word.  
* **In your mouth and heart**:   
  * The law is meant to be internalized—not just known, but lived (anticipates the internal transformation promised in the new covenant; cf. Jer 31:33; Rom 10:6–8).

2. A Clear Covenant Choice: Life or Death (vv.15–18)

> *“I have set before you today life and good, death and evil…”*

* Moses lays out a binary choice with covenant consequences:  
  * Obedience = life, blessing, longevity in the land.  
  * Disobedience = death, curse, destruction, exile from the land.  
* This is rooted in the blessing and curse structure of Deuteronomy (esp. ch. 28).  
* Loving and obeying God is the covenant path to life, while idolatry leads to ruin.

3. Love and Obedience Are Covenantally Linked (v.16)

> *“…by loving the Lord your God, by walking in his ways, and by keeping his commandments…”*

* Love is not abstract—it is shown through obedience.  
* This reflects the biblical principle that obedience is a response to grace, not a means of earning it.  
* The heart of covenant is relational loyalty to God.

4. Warning Against Turning Away (vv.17–18)

* Moses gives a sobering reminder: if the people turn away to worship other gods, they will perish.  
* This reflects the conditionality of the Mosaic covenant in its national, land-centered application.

5. A Final Plea: Choose Life (vv.19–20)

> *“I call heaven and earth to witness… choose life…”*

* A covenant lawsuit formula—calling creation as witness to Israel’s decision.  
* “Choose life” is a pastoral, emotional appeal—Moses pleads for their flourishing.

Key dimensions of choosing life:

* **Loving the Lord**  
* **Obeying His voice**  
* **Holding fast to Him**

> *“For He is your life and length of days…”*

* God Himself is the source of life—not just the rewarder of it.  
* The passage ends by reminding Israel that God is keeping His covenant promises to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

Theological Significance

* **Covenant Accessibility**:   
  * God’s law is not inaccessible or unreasonable—He gives His people everything they need for obedience and life.  
* **Moral Clarity and Responsibility**:   
  * The path is clear—life or death. Israel must respond to God's grace.  
* **Heart-Level Obedience**:   
  * Earlier in Deut 30:6, God promises to circumcise Israel’s heart.  
  * This section assumes the need for heart-level obedience, pointing toward the new covenant (Jer 31:33; Ezek 36:26).  
* **Continuity and Fulfillment in Christ**:  
  * Paul quotes this passage in Romans 10:6–10, applying it to the gospel: *“The word is near you… if you confess… and believe…”*  
  * Jesus is the embodiment of the Word and the one who enables us to choose life.

##### Discussion Questions:

* Why does Moses emphasize the accessibility of God's commands?  
* What does “choosing life” mean practically in today’s context?  
* In what ways do we still experience the blessings and challenges of this covenant framework?  
* What does it mean that God’s word is “not too hard” or “far off”? How is that encouraging?  
* Why is it important that love, not just obedience, is central to covenant life?  
* What makes choosing life difficult in daily practice—even when the choice seems clear?  
* How does this passage connect to Jesus’ invitation in John 10:10 (“I came that they may have life”)?  
* What would it look like this week to “hold fast to God”?

##### Gospel Connection

While the Mosaic law reveals God’s will and points toward righteousness, it also exposes our inability to fully obey. Paul writes in Romans that the law is holy and good, but sin uses it to bring death (Romans 7:10–13). Therefore:

* Jesus perfectly kept the law on our behalf.  
* Jesus bore the curse of our disobedience (Galatians 3:13).  
* Through the Spirit, we are now empowered to walk in righteousness (Romans 8:4).  
* Jesus is the embodiment of God’s nearness—the Word made flesh.  
* Through Him, we are offered life, not by works, but by faith (Rom 10:6–10).  
* He fulfilled the law, bore the curse, and gave the Spirit who writes the law on our hearts (Jer 31:33).  
* In Christ, we are empowered to love, obey, and choose life daily.

#### Reflection & Application

**Personal Reflection Prompts:**

* Which area of God's moral law challenges you most right now?  
* Are you tempted to relate to God through legalism or license?  
* How can the gospel help you obey God with joy rather than fear?

**Group Prayer Ideas:**

* Thank God for revealing His character and desires through His law.  
* Confess areas of spiritual compromise or self-reliance.  
* Ask for the Spirit’s help to live lives marked by holiness and love.

* **Memory Verse:** Deuteronomy 30:19 — *“I have set before you life and death, blessing and curse. Therefore choose life...”*  
* **Reflection Exercise:**  
   Reflect on the Ten Commandments this week. Which command is God calling you to obey more fully right now? Journal a prayer of surrender or repentance related to it.

### Lesson 4.1 – David, Israel, and the Nations

#### Key Texts:

* **2 Samuel 7:1–17** — God’s covenant promise to David  
* **Psalm 2** — The Messianic King and His rule over the nations

**Theme:** God establishes an eternal covenant with David, promising a royal line that ultimately points to Christ, the King of all nations.

#### Part 1: God’s Covenant with David (2 Samuel 7:1–17)

**Read:** 2 Samuel 7:1–17

##### Key Concepts:

1. David’s Desire to Build for God (vv.1–3)

> ***“See now, I dwell in a house of cedar, but the ark of God dwells in a tent.”***

* David expresses his desire to build a house (temple) for God, now that his own house (kingdom) is secure.  
  * His desire is noble, but he assumes he can now repay God for His blessings.  
* Initially, the prophet Nathan affirms the plan—but God redirects it in a stunning way.

2. God's Grace Before David’s Plans (vv.4–7)

> ***“Would you build me a house? … I have not lived in a house since the day I brought up the people of Israel.”***

* God rebukes the assumption that He needs a house—He’s not limited to structures.  
* This reflects God's sovereign freedom and presence, not confined to human efforts.  
* God's focus is not on buildings, but on relationship and mission.  
  * Instead of David building for God, God will build a house (dynasty) for David.

3. God's Covenant with David (vv.8–17)

This is the heart of the passage. God turns David’s plan around: instead of David building God a house, God will build David a “house” (dynasty).

a. Review of Past Grace (v.8–9)

* God reminds David that He chose him from shepherding to rule.  
* He has been with David, giving him victory and renown.  
* The covenant is built on God’s initiative and grace, not David’s merit.

b. Promise of Security and Place for Israel (v.10–11a)

* God promises to secure Israel in peace and permanence.  
* Echoes the Abrahamic promise of land and protection (Gen 12:7; 15:18).

c. Promise of a Dynasty and Eternal Kingdom (vv.11b–16)

This is the covenant core:

* **“The LORD will make you a house” (v.11)**   
  * A royal dynasty.  
* **“I will raise up your offspring… and establish his kingdom” (v.12)**   
  * Refers immediately to Solomon.  
* **“He shall build a house for my name” (v.13)**   
  * Solomon will build the temple.  
* **“I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever”**   
  * Goes beyond Solomon to the Messiah.  
* **“I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son” (v.14)**   
  * Echoes covenantal sonship, picked up in Psalm 2 and Hebrews 1.  
* Even if the king sins, God's steadfast love (hesed) will not depart as it did from Saul (v.15).  
* **“Your house and your kingdom shall be made sure forever”**   
  * A promise of a perpetual throne, fulfilled in Jesus Christ, the Son of David (Luke 1:32–33).

d. Prophetic Authority (v.17)

* Nathan conveys the whole vision—this is not David’s idea, but God’s sovereign covenant initiative.

Theological Significance

* **Covenant Continuity and Expansion**  
  * This covenant builds on the Abrahamic promises: land, offspring, blessing.  
  * It introduces kingship as the means through which those promises will be fulfilled.  
    1. Israel’s hope is no longer just in law or sacrifice, but in a person—a king who rules in righteousness and peace.  
    2. This anticipates the messianic expectation that shapes the prophetic writings and the Gospels.  
* **Messianic Expectation**  
  * The promise of a forever king lays the groundwork for Jesus, the Son of David.  
    1. The promise of an eternal throne transcends Solomon.  
  * The New Testament connects this directly to Christ (Luke 1:32–33, Acts 2:29–36, Romans 1:3).  
* **God’s Initiative and Grace**  
  * Like Abraham and Moses, God initiates the covenant  
    1. David is a recipient, not a negotiator.  
  * David doesn’t earn this covenant  
    1. it’s unilateral and unconditional in its long-term scope.  
  * This reflects God’s ongoing purpose to form a people, bless them, and dwell with them through a king who rules in righteousness.  
* **Sonship and Discipline**  
  * The promise includes fatherly discipline, showing that God’s covenant does not eliminate consequences, but preserves relationships.

##### Discussion Questions:

* Why do you think God said "no" to David's desire to build the temple?  
* What does it mean for God to “build a house” for David?  
* How does this covenant deepen our understanding of kingship in God’s kingdom?

Key ideas:

4. A Call to Wisdom and Surrender (vv.10–12)

Key ideas:

* God’s rule demands a response: submission or judgment.  
* There is refuge and blessing for those who yield to the Son.

Theological & Covenant Significance

* **Christ’s Reign and the Gospel**  
* The psalm calls all people to submit to God’s appointed King—Jesus—who offers both warning and refuge.  
* The gospel is both an invitation to blessing and a warning of judgment.

### Lesson 4.2 – House of the Lord, Everlasting Kingdom

#### Key Texts:

* **Psalm 89** — A reflection on God’s covenant faithfulness to David  
* **Isaiah 9:6–7** — A prophecy of the child who will rule on David’s throne  
* **Luke 1:30–33** — The angel Gabriel’s announcement to Mary of Jesus' eternal kingship

**Theme:** God’s covenant with David points beyond Solomon to a greater King—Jesus—whose reign is eternal and whose kingdom brings peace.

#### Part 1: God's Covenant Reaffirmed and Questioned (Psalm 89)

**Read:** Selected verses from Psalm 89

* **Verses 1–4** — God’s covenant with David  
* **Verses 19–37** — The enduring nature of the promise  
* **Verses 38–52** — A crisis of faith during exile or suffering

##### Key Concepts:

1. God’s Steadfast Love and Faithfulness (vv.1–18)

> ***“I will sing of the steadfast love of the LORD forever…”*** **(v.1)**

* The psalm opens with praise for God's chesed (steadfast love) and emunah (faithfulness).  
* God's character is the foundation of His covenant dealings.  
* God’s power and justice are on display in creation and in His reign:  
  * He rules the sea (v.9)  
  * crushes enemies (v.10)   
  * establishes righteousness and justice (v.14)  
* His people are blessed to walk in His light and rejoice in His name (vv.15–18).

2. God’s Covenant with David (vv.19–37)

> ***“I have made a covenant with my chosen one…”*** **(v.3)**

* Recounts the Davidic Covenant (2 Samuel 7), where God promised:  
  * David’s dynasty would endure forever (v.4, v.29).  
  * His line would rule securely under God’s care.  
  * God would treat the king as His son (v.26–27).  
  * Even if David’s descendants sinned, God would discipline without withdrawing His love (vv.30–33).  
* The covenant is said to be irrevocable:  
  * *“I will not lie to David”* (v.35)  
  * *“His throne shall be established forever”* (v.36)

3. Crisis and Lament: Where Is Your Steadfast Love? (vv.38–52)

> ***“But now you have cast off and rejected…”*** **(v.38)**

* The psalmist shifts dramatically: what God promised doesn’t match what they see.  
* God’s anointed king (David’s heir) has been humiliated and overthrown.  
* Israel faces shame, defeat, and national collapse (likely during or after exile).  
* The psalmist accuses God of breaking His covenant (v.39).  
* Ends with a bold plea:  
   ***“Lord, where is your steadfast love of old…?”* (v.49)**  
  * Appeals to God’s faithfulness and past promises, begging for restoration.

Theological and Covenant Significance

* **Tension Between Promise and Experience**  
  * This psalm uniquely holds both covenant praise and covenant lament in one place.  
  * It reflects the deep theological wrestling of the exilic and post-exilic community.  
  * It asks: *How can God's covenant be true when David’s throne is in ruins.*  
* **Foreshadowing of Messianic Hope**  
  * Though not explicitly messianic, Psalm 89 prepares the way for the longing of a greater Davidic king—one who will truly reign forever.  
  * The New Testament sees Jesus as the fulfillment of the Davidic promises:  
    * *“The root and descendant of David”* (Rev 22:16)  
    * *“Of His kingdom there shall be no end”* (Luke 1:32–33)  
*  **Faith That Laments**  
  * The psalm ends unresolved—not with triumph, but with a faith-filled question.  
  * It gives voice to the faithful who believe in God’s covenant, even in suffering.

##### Gospel Connection

* Jesus is the true and greater Davidic King who never fails (Rev 5:5, Luke 1:32–33).

* Through resurrection, Jesus secures the eternal throne and fulfills Psalm 89’s longing.  
* In Him, God's steadfast love is never withdrawn, even when we don’t see immediate outcomes.

#### Part 2: A Coming King of Peace (Isaiah 9:6–7)

**Read:** Isaiah 9:6–7

##### Key Concepts:

1. A Child Will Be Born, a Son Will Be Given

> ***“For to us a child is born, to us a son is given…”***

* Human and divine tension:   
  * The ruler comes as a child (human), yet He is a gift from God (divine authority).  
* This balances earthly birth with heavenly purpose.  
  * a fulfillment of God’s promise (echoes Isaiah 7:14).  
* The phrasing echoes God’s covenantal gift of a son—especially David’s line (2 Sam 7:14).

2. Authority on His Shoulders

> ***“…and the government shall be upon his shoulder…”***

* Symbolizes the king’s responsibility to rule and carry the burden of governance.  
* This child will bear the full weight of ruling the world—unlike earthly kings who often fail or oppress.

3. Fourfold Name Revealing Divine Identity and Function

> ***“And his name shall be called: Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace.”***

Each name reveals the character and mission of this coming King:

a. Wonderful Counselor

* He rules with wisdom, not manipulation.  
* His counsel is miraculous, wise beyond human reason.

b. Mighty God

* This is a stunning title—a human child is called El Gibbor, “God the Warrior.”  
* Affirms the divine nature of the Messiah.

c. Everlasting Father

* Not referring to the Trinity role, but to His care and protection as a king who acts like a father to His people.  
* Everlasting speaks to the eternal nature of His reign and presence.

d. Prince of Peace

* He will bring shalom—not just the absence of war, but wholeness, justice, and flourishing.  
* His rule restores what sin and war have broken.

4. Expanding Government and Endless Peace

> ***“Of the increase of his government and of peace there will be no end…”***

* His reign will grow continuously, not decline.  
* The peace He brings will be eternal and unshakable, unlike Israel’s fragile kingdoms.

5. Rule on David’s Throne

> *“…on the throne of David and over his kingdom…”*

* Connects directly to the Davidic Covenant (2 Sam 7:12–16).  
* This child will fulfill the promise of a forever king from David’s line.  
* His reign is not political only, but eschatological—God’s ultimate plan for human history.

6. Justice and Righteousness Forever

> ***“…to establish it and uphold it with justice and righteousness…”***

* His kingdom will be marked by moral clarity, fairness, and divine order.  
* In contrast to corrupt rulers, this King rules perfectly, forever.

7. The Zeal of the Lord Will Accomplish It

> ***“…the zeal of the LORD of hosts will do this.”***

* God's passionate commitment guarantees this promise.  
* This kingdom is not built by human effort—it is divine in origin and power.

Theological Significance: 

* **Messianic Fulfillment**  
  * Points directly to Jesus Christ, whose birth, identity, and kingdom fulfill this prophecy (Luke 1:32–33, John 1:14, Revelation 11:15).  
* **Davidic Covenant**  
  * The promised King from David’s line will rule forever (cf. 2 Sam 7). Isaiah 9 shows how this will be fulfilled in an *eternal* and *divine* way.  
* **God’s Sovereignty and Grace**  
  * Salvation and kingdom restoration are God’s doing, not human. His *zeal* drives redemption.  
* **Hope in Darkness**  
  * Originally spoken into a time of political and spiritual crisis, this passage gives hope beyond exile—a vision of peace that sustains the faithful remnant.

### Lesson 4.3 – The Messianic Branch

#### Key Texts:

* **Jeremiah 23:5–6** — A righteous Branch from David will reign as King  
* **Isaiah 11:1–10** — A Spirit-filled descendant of Jesse will restore peace and righteousness

**Theme:** *In the midst of political failure and spiritual darkness, God promises a righteous Branch from David’s line who will bring justice, peace, and hope to all nations.*

#### Part 1: The Righteous Branch from David (Jeremiah 23:5–6)

**Read: Jeremiah 23:5–6**

##### Key Concepts:

1. A Future Hope from David’s Line

> ***“Behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will raise up for David a righteous Branch…”***

* “The days are coming” is a prophetic marker of God’s future redemptive action.  
* The “Branch” imagery echoes Isaiah 11:1 and Zechariah 3:8—indicating a new growth from the “stump” of David’s dynasty, which seemed dead.  
* God will raise up the Branch—this is His initiative, not human restoration.  
  * The image of a Branch implies new life and restoration from what appeared cut off (David’s line looked dead in exile).  
* This affirms the continuity of the Davidic Covenant (2 Sam 7), even in the face of exile and leadership failure.

2. A Righteous King Who Reigns Wisely

> ***“…and he shall reign as king and deal wisely, and shall execute justice and righteousness in the land.”***

* The coming king is described with three key traits:  
  * **Wisdom** – He will rule with divine insight.  
  * **Justice** – He will set things right.  
  * **Righteousness** – His character and decisions will align with God’s will.  
* This is in contrast to the corrupt kings of Jeremiah’s day (see Jer. 22).  
* The reign of this King will embody covenantal ideals: faithfulness to God and care for the people.  
  * The King embodies God’s rule, not merely human authority.

3. Salvation and Security for God’s People

> ***“In his days Judah will be saved, and Israel will dwell securely.”***

* Judah and Israel: a reunified people of God under one righteous King.  
* “Saved” and “dwell securely” are covenantal phrases, tied to God's promises of rest, peace, and protection (Deut. 12:10; Ezek. 34:25–28).  
  * Salvation and safety are marks of the covenant fulfilled (cf. Deut. 12:10; Ezek. 34:25–28).  
* This anticipates both spiritual deliverance and national restoration.  
  * This speaks to both national restoration and deeper spiritual salvation.

4. The King’s Name: “The LORD Is Our Righteousness”

> ***“…And this is the name by which he will be called: ‘The LORD is our righteousness.’”***

* This title (Yahweh Tsidkenu) has profound theological weight:  
  * It declares that God Himself provides righteousness for His people.  
  * In contrast to self-righteousness or legal performance, the king’s very identity is righteousness.  
* In Jesus, this is fulfilled both legally and relationally (cf. 1 Cor 1:30; 2 Cor 5:21).  
* This name may also contrast Zedekiah, the last king before the exile, whose name meant “The Lord is righteous,” but who failed to live it out.

Theological and Covenant Significance

| Theme | Description |
| ----- | ----- |
| **Davidic Covenant Fulfilled** | Reaffirms God’s promise to raise a ruler from David’s line whose reign will be just, wise, and eternal (2 Sam 7). |
| **Righteousness from God** | Emphasizes that salvation and right standing come from God, not human effort—foreshadowing the New Covenant in Christ. |
| **Hope in Crisis** | Given during a time of failed leadership, this prophecy brings hope to the remnant that God is still faithful. |
| **Messianic Expectation** | Builds anticipation for a true king who will save and restore—not by force, but by righteous rule and divine wisdom. |
| **Unity and Restoration** | Suggests a reunified people of God, living securely under the Messiah’s reign. |

##### Gospel Connection

* Jesus is the righteous Branch, the true Son of David, who reigns with justice and brings salvation.  
* He is our righteousness—not only in His rule but in His work on the cross (Rom 3:21–26; 1 Cor 1:30).  
* His reign began with His resurrection and will be completed when He returns to establish perfect peace and justice forever.

#### Part 2: The Shoot from Jesse’s Stump (Isaiah 11:1–10)

**Read: Isaiah 11:1–10**

##### Key Concepts:

1. A Righteous Shoot from Jesse’s Stump

> ***“There shall come forth a shoot from the stump of Jesse, and a branch from his roots shall bear fruit.”*** **(v.1)**

* “Stump of Jesse” signifies the cut-down, humbled state of David’s royal line after exile.  
  * The “stump” represents the devastation of David’s line through judgment and exile.  
* “Shoot” and “branch” imagery conveys renewal and hope—life from apparent death.  
* Highlights the continuity with the Davidic Covenant, while subtly shifting the focus to Jesse, David’s father, suggesting a new beginning.  
  * The reference to Jesse (David’s father) underscores a fresh start, even deeper than David himself.

2. Empowered by the Spirit of the Lord

> ***“The Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him…”*** **(v.2)**

* The coming king will be divinely anointed, not merely appointed.  
  * The King is empowered by the Spirit of the LORD—not political might or military power.  
* He will embody the fullness of God’s Spirit:  
  * Wisdom and understanding – insight into complex matters  
  * Counsel and might – skill in leading and strength to deliver  
  * Knowledge and fear of the Lord – deep relationship with God and reverence  
* This mirrors the ideal king from Deuteronomy 17 and the Servant of the Lord later in Isaiah (Isa 42:1)  
  * He embodies wisdom, understanding, counsel, might, knowledge, and fear of the LORD.  
  * This reflects God’s ideal for covenant leadership (cf. Deut. 17:14–20; Isaiah 9:6–7).

3. Justice for the Poor and Righteous Judgment

> ***“He shall not judge by what his eyes see… but with righteousness he shall judge the poor…”*** **(vv.3–5)**

* His rule will be marked by perfect justice, unlike the corrupt kings of Isaiah’s time.  
* He will defend the vulnerable and strike the wicked—a king who is both compassionate and uncompromising.  
  * He rules with righteous judgment that is not deceived by appearances.  
* Righteousness and faithfulness are not just attributes; they are His clothing—His very nature.

4. Cosmic Peace and Reversal of the Curse

> ***“The wolf shall dwell with the lamb… and a little child shall lead them.”*** **(vv.6–8)**

* Describes a radical transformation of creation—hostility and violence are replaced by peace.  
  * Hostility is reversed: predators and prey dwell together.  
* The imagery echoes Eden, suggesting reversal of the Fall’s effects.  
  * A child leads wild animals—symbolizing innocence and peace.  
* This is not merely poetic: it points to the comprehensive shalom that Messiah brings—physical, social, and spiritual.  
  * This peace is not just political; it's cosmic and creational—a reversal of Genesis 3’s curse.

5. Universal Knowledge of the Lord

> ***“The earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD as the waters cover the sea.”*** **(v.9)**

* The Messiah’s reign will result in universal awareness of and submission to God.  
  * He draws all nations, fulfilling the Abrahamic promise to bless the world (Gen 12:3).  
* It is not just about Israel—but about the whole world living in harmony under God’s rule.

6. A Banner for the Nations

> ***“In that day the root of Jesse… shall stand as a signal for the peoples…”*** **(v.10)**

* The Messiah is both a shoot and a root—He comes from Jesse but also precedes and grounds him (cf. Rev. 22:16).  
* He will be a banner (signal) to which all nations rally—pointing to a global kingdom.  
* This expands the Davidic Covenant to include Gentile inclusion, anticipating the New Covenant reality (Rom. 15:12).

Theological and Covenant Significance

### Lesson 5.1 – The Life, Death, and Resurrection of Jesus

#### Key Texts:

* **Luke 22:14–20** — The Last Supper and institution of the New Covenant  
* **Hebrews 9:11–15** — Jesus as the High Priest of the New Covenant  
* **Romans 6:1–11** — New life through union with Christ in His death and resurrection

**Theme:** Jesus inaugurates the New Covenant through His atoning death and resurrection, bringing forgiveness, freedom, and life to all who believe.

#### Part 1: Jesus Institutes the New Covenant (Luke 22:14–20)

**Read:** Luke 22:14–20

Consider also reading Exodus 12:1–30 (briefly summarized if needed) and Jeremiah 31:31–34 to give covenant context.

##### Key Concepts:

1. The Setting: The Final Passover Meal

> ***“When the hour came, he reclined at table, and the apostles with him.”*** **(v.14)**

* Jesus celebrates Passover, the meal remembering God’s deliverance from Egypt (Exodus 12).  
* This setting connects Jesus' actions to the Exodus covenant and God's saving acts in history.  
* The timing is intentional—Jesus is recasting the meaning of Passover around Himself.  
  * He transforms the meal into a new covenant meal centered on His coming sacrifice.  
  * This marks a turning point: the Exodus redemption is now fulfilled in Him (cf. Luke 9:31, “His exodus”).

2. Eager Desire and Fulfillment (v.15–16)

> ***“I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer...”***

* Jesus expresses deep emotional longing—He knows this is His final meal before death.  
* He says He will not eat it again “until it is fulfilled in the kingdom of God,” showing:  
  * His coming suffering is part of God’s redemptive plan.  
  * The meal anticipates the consummation of God's kingdom, pointing forward to a future messianic banquet (cf. Isaiah 25:6–9).

3. The Cup of Thanksgiving and the Coming Kingdom (v.17–18)

> ***“Take this and divide it among yourselves…”***

* The shared cup reflects fellowship and covenant unity.  
* Jesus links the meal to the arrival of God’s kingdom, signaling a new era is beginning through His death.  
  * His suffering is not the end—but the beginning of God’s final redemptive act.

4. The Bread: His Body Given (v.19)

> ***“This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me.”***

* The bread symbolizes Jesus’ body sacrificed on the cross.  
* “Given for you” reveals His substitutionary role  
  * He dies on behalf of sinners.  
* Jesus redefines remembrance  
  * Not of Egypt and Moses, but of His own sacrifice.  
* This inaugurates a new form of covenant remembrance, centered on Christ.

5. The Cup: The New Covenant in His Blood (v.20)

> ***“This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood.”***

* Direct reference to Jeremiah 31:31–34, where God promised a new covenant marked by:  
  * Internal transformation  
  * Forgiveness of sin  
  * Personal relationship with God  
* “Poured out” echoes Exodus 24:8 (the Mosaic covenant ratified by blood), but now points to Jesus’ blood as the means of covenant ratification.  
  * Jesus becomes both the sacrificial lamb and the covenant mediator (cf. Hebrews 9:15).  
* This is the foundation of the gospel: Jesus’ death secures forgiveness, reconciliation, and access to God.

Theological and Covenant Significance 

* **New Covenant Fulfillment**  
  * Jesus’ words directly reference Jeremiah 31:31–34, showing that His death inaugurates the promised New Covenant.  
  * The New Covenant is not a replacement, but a fulfillment—bringing together the themes of deliverance (Exodus), relationship (Abraham), and kingship (David).  
  * The covenant community now forms around the crucified and risen Christ, not law, ethnicity, or temple.  
* **Substitutionary Atonement**  
  * Jesus offers His body and blood “for you”, making clear that His death is a sacrificial act in place of sinners.  
* **Continuity with the Exodus**  
  * The Last Supper takes place during Passover and reinterprets it—Jesus is now the true Passover Lamb (cf. 1 Cor. 5:7).  
* **Kingdom Inauguration**  
  * Jesus ties the meal to the coming of God’s kingdom, showing that His death launches a new era of redemption.  
* **Covenant Remembrance**  
  * The Lord’s Supper becomes the sign of the New Covenant, just as Passover was for the Old—centered on remembering Christ and His sacrifice.

##### Gospel Connection

* Jesus’ death is the foundation of the New Covenant—the final and eternal covenant in which we are forgiven, indwelt by the Spirit, and made God’s people.  
* His body and blood replace the old system of sacrifices, making once-for-all atonement (Heb. 10:10).  
* When we take the Lord’s Supper, we remember the cross, live under the new covenant, and look forward to the kingdom feast to come.

#### Part 2: A Better Sacrifice, a Better Covenant (Hebrews 9:11–15)

**Read:** Hebrews 9:11–15

For added context, briefly summarize:

The **tabernacle system** (Hebrews 9:1–10)

The **Day of Atonement** (Leviticus 16)

The **New Covenant promise** (Jeremiah 31:31–34)

##### Key Concepts:

1. Christ as the Greater High Priest

> ***“But when Christ appeared as a high priest of the good things that have come…”*** **(v.11)**

* Jesus fulfills the role of High Priest, but not within the earthly tabernacle.  
* He enters the “greater and more perfect tent”—a heavenly reality, not made by human hands.  
  * This marks the transition from shadow to substance in the covenant storyline (cf. Heb. 8:5).  
* His ministry represents the ultimate fulfillment of the old covenant’s priestly system.  
  * These “good things” are the real benefits of the New Covenant—forgiveness, access, transformation.

2. A Better Tabernacle (v.11)

* The earthly tabernacle (and temple) was a copy of heavenly things (cf. Heb. 8:5).  
* Jesus enters the real, eternal dwelling of God, not the shadow.  
* This shift signals the New Covenant age, where access to God is direct and permanent.

3. Blood of Christ vs. Animal Sacrifices

> ***“…not by means of the blood of goats and calves but by means of his own blood…”*** **(v.12)**

* Under the old covenant, animal blood was used repeatedly for ritual purification.  
* Jesus brings His own blood, a once-for-all sacrifice that truly cleanses.  
  * His blood actually accomplishes what others could only symbolize: full, eternal redemption.  
* This demonstrates the superiority and sufficiency of Christ’s death.

4. Eternal Redemption Secured

> ***“…thus securing an eternal redemption.”*** **(v.12)**

* Christ’s sacrifice doesn’t just cover sin temporarily—it purchases full and eternal salvation.  
* This contrasts with the repeated, limited sacrifices of the old system.  
* “Eternal” reflects both the quality and permanence of the redemption Jesus provides.

5. Cleansing the Conscience

> ***“…how much more will the blood of Christ…purify our conscience from dead works to serve the living God.”*** **(v.14)**

* The old covenant rituals could cleanse the body, but not the conscience.  
* Jesus' blood purifies at the deepest level, freeing people from guilt and performance-based religion.  
* This enables true worship and service, not fear or obligation.

6. Christ as Mediator of a New Covenant

> ***“Therefore he is the mediator of a new covenant…”*** **(v.15)**

* Jesus not only offers a better sacrifice, but mediates a better covenant (cf. Heb. 8:6).  
* Through His death, believers receive the promised eternal inheritance.  
* His death redeems from transgressions committed under the first covenant—He fulfills the law’s demands and its penalties.  
  * His sacrifice redeems people under the old covenant and secures the eternal inheritance promised to all God’s people.

Theological and Covenant Significance

* **Jesus Fulfills and Surpasses the Old Covenant**:   
  * He serves as both priest and sacrifice, bringing access to God that the old system could only point to.  
* **His Blood Establishes the New Covenant**:   
  * Unlike animal sacrifices, His blood secures forgiveness, inner cleansing, and lasting redemption.  
* **True Access to God Is Achieved**:   
  * Through Jesus’ priesthood, believers now approach God directly, freely, and confidently.  
* **The New Covenant Is Based on Eternal Realities**:   
  * Jesus’ work happens in the heavenly tabernacle, securing eternal benefits for His people.  
* **Conscience and Worship Are Transformed**:   
  * Instead of dead religion, believers now serve God with clear consciences and new hearts, just as promised in Jeremiah 31.

* Why does the author emphasize that Jesus entered the “greater and more perfect tent”? What does this say about His priesthood?  
* How is Jesus’ sacrifice different from those under the old covenant?

##### Gospel Connection

* Jesus is the true and final High Priest, whose perfect sacrifice brings us full forgiveness and eternal life.  
* The New Covenant is built not on law or temple, but on His blood.  
* His work on the cross means we now live with access to God, cleansed hearts, and the hope of eternal inheritance.  
* The gospel is the New Covenant promise fulfilled: *God with us, sin forgiven, hearts made new*.

#### Part 3: New Life Through Resurrection (Romans 6:1–11)

**Read:** Romans 6:1–11

Consider briefly reviewing the context of Romans 5:12–21 (Adam and Christ) to set the stage.

##### Key Concepts:

1. Grace Is Not a License to Sin

> ***“What shall we say then? Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound?”*** **(v.1)**

* Paul confronts a misunderstanding: that more sin means more grace.  
* He insists that grace transforms, not excuses, sinful living.  
* The logic of salvation by grace leads to sanctification, not spiritual laziness.  
  * Grace leads to transformation, not indulgence.

2. Union with Christ in His Death (vv.2–3)

> ***“Do you not know that all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death?”***

* Paul explains that believers are spiritually united with Christ.  
  * We are spiritually joined to His death and resurrection.  
* Baptism symbolizes this union: being buried with Christ.  
  * This union is not metaphorical—it is foundational to our new identity in the New Covenant.  
* This means we are dead to sin—its reign is broken.

3. Raised to New Life with Christ (v.4–5)

> ***“…so that just as Christ was raised… we too might walk in newness of life.”***

* The resurrection isn't just about future hope—it's about present transformation.  
* Being raised with Christ means we now live under the power of new life.  
* This connects to the New Covenant promise of inner renewal (Jer. 31:33; Ezek. 36:26).

4. Old Self Crucified, Body of Sin Brought to Nothing (v.6)

> ***“…our old self was crucified with him… so that we would no longer be enslaved to sin.”***

* “Old self” refers to who we were in Adam—dominated by sin.  
* That person was crucified with Christ, and sin’s enslaving power was broken.  
* We are now free to live for God under the reign of grace (cf. Rom. 5:21).

5. Freed from Sin’s Dominion (vv.7–10)

> ***“For one who has died has been set free from sin.”***

* Death breaks the legal claim of sin over a person.  
* Christ died once for all, never to die again—He now lives to God.  
  * Just as Christ now lives to God, so do we.  
* Likewise, we now live under a new authority: not sin, but God’s righteousness.  
  * This reflects the New Covenant promises of Ezekiel 36—where God gives a new heart and Spirit to walk in His ways.

6. Consider Yourself Dead to Sin, Alive to God (v.11)

> ***“So you also must consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus.”***

* This is the first imperative in Romans: “Consider” (reckon) yourself this way.  
* It’s not a call to strive, but to believe and act according to a new reality.  
  * This is a call to live in light of our true identity.  
  * In the New Covenant, transformation begins with new status and leads to new living.  
* Identity shapes behavior—who you are in Christ determines how you live.

Theological and Covenant Significance

* **Union with Christ**  
  * Believers are united to Jesus in His death and resurrection. This union is foundational to the New Covenant, where we are made new creations.  
  * Romans 6 shows that believers now live in a new realm—no longer in Adam, but in Christ.  
* **Freedom from Sin’s Power**  
  * The cross doesn’t just forgive—it liberates. Sin is no longer the master; believers live under grace’s reign (Rom. 6:14).  
  * Living in sin would be like returning to slavery after being set free—an affront to the covenant’s intent.  
* **Newness of Life**  
  * The resurrection means we participate in a new way of living—a life of righteousness, empowered by the Spirit (Rom. 8).  
* **Identity-Based Transformation**  
  * Sanctification begins by recognizing our new identity in Christ—dead to sin, alive to God.  
  * The grace that saves also sanctifies—we are changed at the root, not just cleaned on the surface.  
* **New Covenant Heart Change**  
  * Echoes Ezekiel 36:26–27, where God promises a new heart and His Spirit to cause His people to walk in His ways.

### Lesson 5.2 – New Creation, New Exodus, New David

#### Key Texts:

* **Ezekiel 36:24–28** — A promise of heart renewal and Spirit empowerment  
* **Revelation 21:1–5** — The vision of new creation and final covenant fulfillment

**Theme:** The New Covenant promises heart transformation, Spirit-empowered living, and the ultimate restoration of creation under Jesus, the true Davidic King.

#### Part 1: Heart of Stone to Heart of Flesh (Ezekiel 36:24–28)

**Read: Ezekiel 36:24–28**

Optionally, review the context: Israel has profaned God’s name through persistent unfaithfulness (vv.16–23), and God now promises a radical act of restoration.

##### Key Concepts:

1. Divine Regathering and Restoration (v.24)

> ***“I will take you from the nations and gather you from all the countries and bring you into your own land.”***

* God promises to regather His scattered people, reversing the judgment of exile.  
* This is not merely a political return but signifies restoration of relationship.  
* It echoes earlier covenant promises (e.g., Deut. 30:1–5), showing God’s covenant faithfulness despite Israel’s unfaithfulness.

2. Spiritual Cleansing (v.25)

> ***“I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean…”***

* The language of sprinkling recalls Levitical purification rituals (e.g., Num. 19:17–19).  
* This cleansing is not just ritual—it addresses defilement from idolatry and sin.  
* It’s God’s initiative: “I will… you shall…”—a unilateral act of grace.

3. New Heart and New Spirit (v.26)

> ***“I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you.”***

* This is internal transformation, not just external obedience.  
* The “heart of stone” represents rebellion and spiritual deadness.  
  * **“Heart of stone”** = hardened, unresponsive, spiritually dead.  
* God promises a heart of flesh—tender, responsive, and alive to Him.  
  * **“Heart of flesh”** = alive, soft, and responsive to God.

4. Indwelling of the Holy Spirit (v.27)

> ***“I will put my Spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes…”***

* God’s Spirit indwelling His people is the key to real change.  
* Obedience flows from transformation—not law, fear, or pressure, but from the Spirit’s power.  
  * The law is no longer an external constraint, but internal desire (cf. Jer. 31:33, Rom. 8:4).  
* This is the essence of the New Covenant (cf. Jer. 31:31–34; Rom. 8:1–4; Gal. 5:16–25).

5. Renewed Covenant Relationship (v.28)

> ***“You shall be my people, and I will be your God.”***

* This formula echoes the core promise of every biblical covenant (cf. Gen. 17:7; Exod. 6:7; Jer. 31:33).  
* Through the New Covenant, God restores full fellowship with His people.  
  * The New Covenant restores intimacy and identity that sin fractured.  
  * It fulfills promises made to Abraham (Gen. 17:7), Moses (Ex. 6:7), and David (2 Sam. 7).  
* It’s a relational promise—not just national restoration, but spiritual communion.

Theological and Covenant Significance

* **God as Initiator:**   
  * Every promise here begins with “I will…”—highlighting divine grace and initiative, not human achievement.  
  * Ezekiel 36 shows that God is reclaiming a people for Himself, not by law, but by grace and transformation.  
* **Cleansing from Sin:**   
  * God addresses both guilt and pollution, fulfilling the priestly role of atonement in a deeper, permanent way.  
* **Heart Transformation:**   
  * Unlike the Old Covenant, which was external and written on stone, the New Covenant transforms the inner person (cf. 2 Cor. 3:3).  
* **The Spirit as Empowerment:**   
  * True obedience comes from God’s Spirit within, not legal effort—this is the New Covenant’s distinguishing mark.  
* **Covenant Fulfillment and Unity:**   
  * The passage ties together the Abrahamic, Mosaic, and Davidic promises in a new, Spirit-empowered relationship.  
  * This passage forms the bridge between the old, broken covenant and the Christ-centered New Covenant of Luke 22 and Hebrews 8–10.

* How do you see this promise fulfilled in the New Testament—in Jesus and in the church?  
* What does the phrase “You shall be my people, and I will be your God” mean to you personally?

##### Gospel Connection:

* Jesus is the fulfillment of this promise: He cleanses by His blood, gives new hearts, and pours out the Spirit (cf. Titus 3:5–6).  
* Through Christ, believers experience the full New Covenant reality—justification, transformation, and relationship.  
* The gospel is not just about being saved from sin’s penalty—it’s about being made new for life with God.

#### Part 2: A New Heaven and New Earth (Revelation 21:1–5)

**Read: Revelation 21:1–5**

Optionally, give a brief context:

* Revelation has shown the judgment of evil, and now the final vision opens—a renewed cosmos and a redeemed people with God.

##### Key Concepts:

1. A New Heaven and a New Earth (v.1)

> ***“Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth, for the first heaven and the first earth had passed away…”***

* This marks the final stage of God’s redemptive plan—the complete renewal of creation.  
* “New” (Greek: *kainos*) means renewed or transformed, not simply brand new from scratch.  
  * Creation is not discarded, but redeemed—echoing the hope of the New Covenant that includes restoration of all things.  
* It echoes Isaiah 65:17 and 2 Peter 3:13, pointing to a world without sin, suffering, or decay.

2. No More Sea

> ***“…and the sea was no more.”***

* In biblical imagery, the sea often represents chaos, evil, or separation (cf. Rev. 13:1).  
* This signifies that in the new creation, all chaos, danger, and disorder are forever gone.

3. The New Jerusalem: God’s Dwelling with Man (v.2–3)

> ***“Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man.”***

* The New Jerusalem, described as a bride adorned for her husband, symbolizes God’s redeemed people and His presence among them.  
* This fulfills the core covenant promise: *“I will be their God, and they will be my people”* (cf. Exod. 6:7; Lev. 26:11–12; Ezek. 37:27).  
* God’s dwelling (Greek: *skēnē*, “tabernacle”) is now permanent and direct—no more temple, veil, or distance.

4. The End of Suffering and Death (v.4)

> ***“He will wipe away every tear from their eyes…”***

* All effects of the fall—death, mourning, crying, and pain—are eradicated forever.  
* This fulfills Isaiah 25:8 and 1 Corinthians 15:26 (“The last enemy to be destroyed is death”).  
* It's the full reversal of the curse from Genesis 3.  
  * This is the true sabbath rest and peace promised in the covenant storyline (cf. Isa. 25:8).

5. God Makes All Things New (v.5)

> ***“Behold, I am making all things new.”***

* God’s redemptive purpose is not to abandon creation, but to renew and restore it.  
* This is the fulfillment of the New Covenant’s promise of renewal (cf. Ezek. 36:26–27).  
* The statement “Write this down” affirms its certainty and importance—it is as good as done.

Theological and Covenant Significance

* **Covenant Fulfillment:**   
  * This is the culmination of all biblical covenants—from Eden, Abraham, Sinai, David, to the New Covenant—bringing God’s people into perfect fellowship with Him.  
  * From Adam (creation), to Abraham (land and blessing), to Moses (obedience and presence), to David (kingdom), and finally to Christ (New Covenant)—everything culminates here.  
* **God Dwelling with His People:**   
  * The goal of the covenant storyline (cf. Lev. 26:11–12, Ezek. 37:27, Rev. 21:3) is now realized permanently and fully.  
* **New Creation:**   
  * God doesn’t discard creation—He redeems and transforms it, just as He does with His people. This reflects Romans 8:18–25 and Isaiah 65.  
  * Revelation 21 shows that the goal of covenant is not just salvation from sin, but eternal communion with God in a renewed world.  
* **No More Death or Curse:**   
  * The curse of Genesis 3 is undone—God’s redemptive story comes full circle.  
* **Eternal Security and Joy:**   
  * This vision provides hope, comfort, and certainty for God’s people in the midst of present trials.

##### Discussion Questions:

* How does the vision of new creation fulfill the hopes of the Old Testament covenants?  
* What emotions or longings does this passage stir in you?

#### Gospel Connection

Jesus is the New David (Luke 1:32), the true Passover Lamb (1 Corinthians 5:7), and the firstborn of new creation (Colossians 1:18). Through His death and resurrection, He:

* Delivers us from slavery to sin (New Exodus)  
* Pours out the Spirit to renew our hearts (New Covenant)  
* Prepares a place in the new creation for His people (New Heaven & Earth)  
* This vision is only possible because of Christ, the mediator of the New Covenant, who:  
* Bore the curse (Gal. 3:13)  
* Rose to conquer death (1 Cor. 15:54)  
* Poured out the Spirit to begin the renewal (Titus 3:5)  
  The gospel is the invitation into this new world—by grace, through faith, into eternal fellowship with God.

This is the gospel in full dimension: personal salvation, relational restoration, communal transformation, and cosmic renewal.

### Lesson 5.3 – Followers of ‘The Way’

#### Key Texts:

* **Acts 2:36–47** — The birth of the New Covenant community  
* **Hebrews 10:14–25** — Assurance, encouragement, and perseverance as covenant people

**Theme:** The New Covenant creates a Spirit-filled, Christ-centered community of believers who live together as the people of God—marked by worship, obedience, and love.

#### Part 1: Life Together in the Spirit (Acts 2:36–47)

**Read: Acts 2:36–47**

Optionally review the context:

* This is the end of Peter’s sermon at Pentecost. The Spirit has been poured out (Acts 2:1–35), and this passage shows how people respond—and how the church is born.

##### Key Concepts:

1. Jesus Declared Lord and Messiah (v.36)

> ***“God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified.”***

* Peter declares that Jesus’ resurrection and exaltation prove His identity as Messiah (Christ) and Lord (King).  
  * Jesus, crucified and raised, now reigns as Davidic King and promised Messiah.  
  * This fulfills the Davidic covenant (2 Sam. 7) and confirms Jesus as the center of God’s redemptive plan.  
* The crowd is confronted with their complicity in His death, and the divine authority He now holds.

2. Conviction and Repentance (vv.37–38)

> ***“They were cut to the heart…”***

* The people respond with deep conviction, asking what they must do.  
* Peter calls for repentance and baptism—a sign of personal faith and public identification with Christ.  
  * Baptism functions as entry into the New Covenant community, symbolizing cleansing and renewal (cf. Ezek. 36:25–27).  
* He promises forgiveness of sins and the gift of the Holy Spirit, echoing New Covenant promises (cf. Ezek. 36:25–27).  
  * The gift of the Holy Spirit fulfills the New Covenant promises (Jer. 31, Joel 2) and empowers obedience and unity.

3. The Promise to All Generations (v.39)

> ***“For the promise is for you and for your children and for all who are far off…”***

* This promise is inclusive, expanding beyond ethnic Israel.  
* It connects to God’s covenant with Abraham: blessing to all nations (Gen. 12:3).  
* The church is now the multiethnic, Spirit-filled people of God.

4. A New Covenant Community Is Born (vv.40–41)

> ***“About three thousand souls were added…”***

* Those who receive the word are baptized and added to the community.  
* This marks the beginning of the church, the new people of God, shaped by faith, grace, and the Spirit.

5. Devoted Fellowship and Life Together (vv.42–47)

> ***“They devoted themselves to the apostles’ teaching and the fellowship…”***

The early church is characterized by five key practices:

1. **Apostolic Teaching**  
    – Rooted in the gospel of Christ and the unfolding of Scripture.  
2. **Fellowship (koinonia)**  
    – Deep, sacrificial community, bound by shared life in Christ.  
3. **Breaking of Bread**  
    – Likely both communal meals and the Lord’s Supper—a sign of covenant renewal.  
4. **Prayer**  
    – Communal dependence on God, shaped by awe and gratitude.  
5. **Generosity and Sharing**  
    – Voluntary care for one another, fulfilling Deuteronomy 15 ideals of no needy among God’s people.  
6. **The result?** Joy, unity, growth, and favor with outsiders—a living picture of the kingdom community.

Theological and Covenant Significance

* **Jesus as Exalted Lord and Christ**  
  * Fulfills the Davidic covenant (2 Sam. 7; Psalm 2) by ascending to the throne as God’s anointed King.  
* **New Covenant Realized**  
  * Forgiveness of sins and the gift of the Spirit fulfill Ezekiel 36 and Jeremiah 31.  
  * Fulfillment of the New Covenant (Jer. 31, Ezek. 36): hearts renewed, sins forgiven, Spirit given.  
* **Baptism as Covenant Entry**  
  * Like circumcision under the Abrahamic covenant, baptism signifies entrance into the covenant community—but now through grace and faith, not ethnicity or law.  
* **Spirit-Empowered Community**  
  * The church reflects God’s restored people, living in covenant love, generosity, and unity.  
  *  This community lives out what the law could never produce: joyful obedience, unity, worship, and mutual love.  
* **Mission and Expansion**  
  * The gospel promise is for “all who are far off”—fulfilling God’s promise to bless the nations through His people.  
  * The community life of the early church is a signpost of the kingdom and a preview of the new creation.

##### Discussion Questions:

* In what ways is the church today similar to or different from this model?

* How does Peter’s declaration of Jesus as “Lord and Christ” change the way we think about His role today?  
* What does baptism represent in this passage? How does it reflect covenant entry?  
* How do you see the Spirit shaping this new community? What stands out to you?  
* Which practices of the early church most challenge or inspire you?  
* How can your own community reflect the same devotion, generosity, and joy?

##### Gospel Connection

* Jesus died and rose again to bring us into a New Covenant, not just with God, but with each other.  
* The gospel doesn’t just save individuals—it forms a family, a people for God’s own possession.  
* Through repentance, baptism, and the Spirit, we become part of God’s renewed people, living for His kingdom.

#### Part 2: The Confidence and Commitment of a Covenant People (Hebrews 10:14–25)

**Read: Hebrews 10:14–25**

Consider assigning multiple readers: one for verses 14–18 (New Covenant fulfillment), another for 19–25 (our response).

##### Key Concepts:

1. Perfected Forever by One Sacrifice (v.14)

> ***“For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified.”***

* Christ’s death completes what animal sacrifices could never do.  
  * The old covenant required endless sacrifices.  
* Believers are both perfected positionally and being sanctified progressively.  
* This is the foundation of New Covenant grace  
  * We are secure because of His finished work.

2. Fulfillment of the New Covenant Promise (vv.15–17)

> ***“…I will put my laws on their hearts… I will remember their sins no more.”***

* The author quotes Jeremiah 31, confirming that Jesus fulfills the New Covenant.  
* God writes His law internally, not just externally.  
  * God changes our hearts and remembers our sin no more—this is the heart of grace.  
* Forgiveness is complete—no more offerings are needed.  
  * Forgiveness is final; we don’t add to Christ’s work.

3. Access to God Is Now Open (vv.19–21)

> ***“We have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus…”***

* Under the Old Covenant, access to God’s presence was limited and fearful.  
* Now, through Christ, believers have bold access to God’s presence.  
  * God has opened the Holy of Holies—we now approach Him with confidence.  
* Jesus is the new and living way and our Great High Priest.  
  * We are cleansed and welcomed, not by law-keeping, but by Christ’s blood.

4. Let Us Draw Near (v.22)

> ***“…with a true heart in full assurance of faith…”***

* This is the first of three “Let us” exhortations.  
* We are invited to draw near in worship, because we are cleansed and accepted.  
* Assurance flows not from self, but from Christ’s blood and priesthood.

5. Let Us Hold Fast Our Hope (v.23)

> ***“…without wavering, for he who promised is faithful.”***

* Faith in Christ leads to hope, even in hardship.  
  * Life in covenant with God includes faithful perseverance.  
* God’s covenantal faithfulness grounds our perseverance.  
  * We cling to hope because He is faithful, not because we are strong.  
* In contrast to the fading old covenant, the New Covenant is unchanging and trustworthy.

6. Let Us Stir One Another Up in Love and Good Works (v.24–25)

> ***“…not neglecting to meet together… but encouraging one another…”***

* Community life is essential under the New Covenant.  
  * The New Covenant community is not just personal—it is communal.  
* Believers are called to encourage, gather, and spur one another on in love and holiness.  
* As “the Day” draws near (Christ’s return), mutual encouragement becomes more vital.  
  * This is especially urgent as we await Christ’s return—the final consummation of the covenant.

Theological and Covenant Significance

* **Christ's Sacrifice Ends the Old System**  
  * Christ is the perfect mediator who accomplishes what all prior covenants pointed toward.  
  * His once-for-all offering replaces repeated sacrifices (cf. Heb. 9:12).  
* **The New Covenant Is Fulfilled**  
  * God forgives sin fully and transforms hearts internally (Jer. 31:31–34).  
* **Free Access to God**  
  * The veil is torn; we now approach God boldly and confidently.  
* **Gospel-Shaped Living**   
  * Faith, hope, and love flow from covenantal security, not obligation.  
* **The Church as a Covenant Community**  
  * Believers live out the covenant together—encouraging, meeting, growing until Christ returns.  
  * We live between the inauguration and consummation of this covenant—awaiting the day when all is made new (cf. Rev. 21).
